1. Respiratory

Airway (upper = stridor; lower = wheeze) vs parenchymal/pleural disease — viruses cause 80–90%.

Introduction: Respiratory System & Respiratory Infections

Definition Upper (extrathoracic) obstruction → inspiratory stridor; lower (intrathoracic) → expiratory wheeze; right lung = 3 lobes (55% volume).
MCQ pearls
  • Cough clue: barking = croup; whooping = pertussis; staccato = Chlamydia; sudden choking = FB; nocturnal = asthma.
  • Respiratory disorders = 50% of illness <5 y; viruses 80–90%; asthma = MC chronic childhood illness.
  • Tachypnea cutoffs: <2 mo >60; 2 mo–1 y >50; 1–5 y >40; 5–9 y >30.
  • Strep pharyngitis → penicillin ×10 d (prevents rheumatic fever); palate petechiae = strep.
  • Sinusitis/AOM bacterial MC = S. pneumoniae; tx high-dose amoxicillin. Tongue depressor contraindicated if airway obstruction suspected.

  • Chlamydia (1–3 mo): afebrile + conjunctivitis + staccato cough + eosinophilia → macrolide.

  • S. aureus (<1 y): toxic, high fever, pneumatocele + empyema → cloxacillin/vancomycin.
  • Kerosene/hydrocarbon ingestion → NO gastric lavage (supportive only).

Test Yourself — Respiratory

Q1. A 4-year-old boy has a 2-day coryzal prodrome followed by a barking cough, hoarse voice, and inspiratory stridor worse at night. He is mildly unwell, afebrile-to-low-grade, and drinking normally. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Acute epiglottitis
  2. Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis)
  3. Bacterial tracheitis
  4. Foreign body aspiration
Answer: B. Gradual onset, coryza prodrome, barking cough, hoarse voice, and ability to drink = viral croup (parainfluenza). Epiglottitis (A) is sudden, toxic, drooling, and unable to drink with a muffled voice.

Q2. A 6-month-old presents in winter with 2 days of coryza then cough, wheeze, poor feeding and fast breathing. Exam: bilateral fine crackles and wheeze, retractions, SpO₂ 94%, no stridor. There is no family history of atopy. Which statement about management is correct?

  1. Nebulised corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment
  2. Oral antibiotics should be started empirically
  3. Management is mainly supportive (hydration, oxygen, monitoring)
  4. A chest X-ray is mandatory to confirm the diagnosis
Answer: C. This is RSV bronchiolitis (<2 y, winter, 1st wheeze, no atopy) — diagnosed clinically and treated supportively. Steroids (A), antibiotics (B), and routine CXR (D) have no proven role.

Q3. A previously well 18-month-old suddenly develops choking and paroxysmal coughing while eating nuts. He stabilises but has reduced air entry over the right lung. Expiratory chest X-ray shows hyperinflation of the right lung. What is the most appropriate definitive step?

  1. Rigid bronchoscopy
  2. A course of oral amoxicillin
  3. Nebulised salbutamol and discharge
  4. CT chest then observation
Answer: A. Choking on nuts + unilateral signs + ipsilateral air-trapping (ball-valve) = foreign body aspiration; rigid bronchoscopy is both diagnostic and the definitive therapeutic removal. Antibiotics/bronchodilators (B, C) do not address the obstruction.

Q4. A 2-month-old afebrile infant has tachypnea, a staccato cough, and conjunctivitis. Bloods show eosinophilia. The mother had an untreated genital infection. Which is the most likely organism and treatment?

  1. Group B streptococcus — IV ampicillin + gentamicin
  2. Respiratory syncytial virus — supportive care
  3. Chlamydia trachomatis — a macrolide
  4. Staphylococcus aureus — cloxacillin
Answer: C. Afebrile pneumonia at 1–3 months with staccato cough, conjunctivitis, and eosinophilia is classic for C. trachomatis → macrolide. GBS (A) causes toxic neonatal pneumonia; S. aureus (D) is sudden, high-fever and toxic with pneumatoceles.

Q5. A 14-year-old with known asthma is brought in with a severe attack. After initial bronchodilators he becomes drowsy with a silent chest. An ABG shows a "normalising" PaCO₂ after being low earlier. What does this indicate?

  1. The attack is resolving and he can be discharged
  2. Impending respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation
  3. A simple respiratory alkalosis
  4. Mild exacerbation needing only continued SABA
Answer: B. A silent chest plus a rising/normalising PaCO₂ in a tiring asthmatic signals impending respiratory failure — ventilate. Early asthma gives respiratory alkalosis (C); the loss of that low CO₂ is the danger sign, not reassurance (A, D).

Test Yourself — Cardiology

Q1. A 4-year-old is referred for a murmur found at a routine check. He is thriving and asymptomatic. Auscultation reveals a fixed, widely split second heart sound with a soft ejection systolic murmur at the upper left sternal edge. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Ventricular septal defect
  2. Atrial septal defect
  3. Patent ductus arteriosus
  4. Tetralogy of Fallot
Answer: B. A fixed wide split S2 is pathognomonic for ASD (the defect itself is silent; the murmur is from increased pulmonary flow). VSD gives a pansystolic murmur at the LLSE and PDA a continuous machinery murmur; TOF would be cyanotic.

Q2. A 3-day-old term neonate collapses with poor perfusion, weak femoral pulses, and severe metabolic acidosis just as the ductus arteriosus is closing. Blood pressure is markedly higher in the arms than the legs. What is the most appropriate immediate management?

  1. Start a prostaglandin E1 infusion
  2. Give intravenous indomethacin
  3. Give intravenous adenosine
  4. Arrange immediate device closure
Answer: A. This is severe (duct-dependent) coarctation — upper-limb BP > lower-limb — presenting at duct closure. PGE1 reopens the duct to restore lower-body perfusion before surgery. Indomethacin would close the duct (lethal here); adenosine treats SVT.

Q3. A 5-month-old with known cyanotic heart disease becomes acutely blue and breathless while crying, then squats into a knee-chest position; his previously loud systolic murmur becomes quieter. Which lesion best explains this episode?

  1. Transposition of the great arteries
  2. Large ventricular septal defect
  3. Tetralogy of Fallot
  4. Total anomalous pulmonary venous return
Answer: C. A tet spell in TOF: infundibular spasm increases R→L shunt, so cyanosis worsens and the pulmonary-stenosis murmur softens; squatting raises SVR and reduces the shunt. TGA presents with day-1 cyanosis and no murmur.

Q4. A 10-year-old girl presents with migratory large-joint arthritis, a new pansystolic murmur radiating to the axilla, and a raised ASO titre three weeks after a sore throat. Which is the single most important long-term intervention?

  1. Lifelong aspirin
  2. Annual echocardiography only
  3. Regular IM benzathine penicillin prophylaxis
  4. Mitral valve replacement now
Answer: C. This is rheumatic fever with carditis (JONES + recent GAS, mitral involvement). Secondary prophylaxis with IM benzathine penicillin prevents recurrent attacks and progressive valve damage. Aspirin treats acute arthritis only; surgery is not indicated acutely.

Q5. A cyanotic neonate has oxygen saturations that fail to rise after 10 minutes in 100% oxygen. The chest X-ray shows a boot-shaped heart with oligemic lung fields. Which finding fits this diagnosis?

  1. Continuous machinery murmur
  2. Fixed wide split S2
  3. Bounding pulses with wide pulse pressure
  4. Single S2 with a loud ejection systolic murmur
Answer: D. A positive hyperoxia test confirms cyanotic CHD, and a boot-shaped, oligemic CXR is classic TOF, which gives a single S2 (absent pulmonary component) and a loud ESM from the pulmonary stenosis. The other options point to PDA, ASD, and PDA respectively.

Test Yourself — Gastrointestinal

Q1. A 6-month-old boy, 7 kg, has had 8 watery stools/day for 2 days. He is lethargic, has sunken eyes, capillary refill of 4 seconds and is hypotensive. What is the most appropriate first step?

  1. Oral rehydration solution 50 mL/kg over 4 hours
  2. IV bolus 20 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl over 20 minutes
  3. Start oral zinc and probiotics
  4. Maintenance fluids only at 100-50-20
Answer: B. This is severe dehydration with shock — give a 20 mL/kg bolus over 20 min. ORS (A) is contraindicated in shock; maintenance alone (D) ignores the deficit.

Q2. A 4-year-old develops bloody diarrhea after eating undercooked beef. Three days later he is pale, oliguric, with platelets 40,000 and rising creatinine. Which is the most appropriate management point?

  1. Start ciprofloxacin immediately
  2. Give an antidiarrheal to slow stool frequency
  3. Avoid antibiotics as they may worsen the condition
  4. Begin metronidazole for presumed amoebiasis
Answer: C. This is HUS from EHEC O157:H7 — antibiotics increase Shiga-toxin release and worsen HUS. Antidiarrheals (B) are not recommended in children.

Q3. A 3-week-old firstborn boy has progressive non-bilious projectile vomiting and remains hungry after vomiting. A 2 cm olive-shaped mass is felt in the epigastrium. ABG shows pH 7.52, low Cl and low K. What is the next best step?

  1. Immediate pyloromyotomy
  2. Upper GI contrast study to exclude volvulus
  3. Correct fluid and electrolyte imbalance, then pyloromyotomy
  4. Start a proton pump inhibitor for reflux
Answer: C. Pyloric stenosis with hypokalemic hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis — correct electrolytes/fluids first, then operate. Surgery before correction (A) is unsafe; vomiting is non-bilious, against volvulus (B).

Q4. A 9-month-old has episodes of colicky pain with drawing-up of the legs, becoming lethargic between attacks, and passes stool mixed with blood and mucus. A sausage-shaped mass is palpable. Which investigation is the best initial test?

  1. Abdominal ultrasound showing a target/doughnut sign
  2. Suction rectal biopsy
  3. 24-hour pH monitoring
  4. Anti-tissue transglutaminase antibody
Answer: A. Intussusception — US is the best initial test (target/doughnut sign); air enema is confirmatory and therapeutic. Rectal biopsy (B) is for Hirschsprung; tTG (D) is for celiac.

Q5. A 15-month-old presents 3 months after introduction of wheat with bulky pale offensive stools, abdominal distension, irritability and falling weight. Which combination is most appropriate for diagnosis?

  1. Stool culture and empirical antibiotics
  2. Anti-tTG IgA with serum IgA, then duodenal biopsy
  3. Sweat chloride test
  4. Hydrogen breath test alone
Answer: B. Classic celiac disease — anti-tTG IgA is the initial test (measure serum IgA to exclude deficiency), with duodenal biopsy as the gold standard. Hydrogen breath test (D) tests lactose intolerance.

Test Yourself — Neurology

Q1. An 8-month-old presents after a generalized tonic-clonic seizure lasting 5 minutes during a fever from roseola. He is now alert, smiling and feeding. Examination is normal. What is the most appropriate next step?

  2. Start prophylactic valproate
  3. Lumbar puncture to exclude meningitis
  4. Urgent CT brain and EEG
Answer: C. Although it looks like a simple febrile seizure, age <12 months mandates LP to exclude meningitis. CT/EEG are not indicated in simple febrile seizures, and prophylactic AEDs don't reduce recurrence.

Q2. A 4-year-old with fever, headache and neck stiffness has CSF showing turbid fluid, WBC >1000 (neutrophil-predominant), protein 180 mg/dL and glucose 25 mg/dL. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Viral (enteroviral) meningitis
  2. Bacterial meningitis
  3. Tuberculous meningitis
  4. Guillain-Barré syndrome
Answer: B. Neutrophilic pleocytosis with high protein and low glucose in turbid CSF is classic bacterial meningitis. Viral shows lymphocytes with normal glucose; TB shows lymphocytes with very high protein and a more chronic course.

Q3. A previously well 6-year-old develops symmetrical ascending leg weakness with absent reflexes two weeks after gastroenteritis. CSF shows raised protein with normal cell count. Which is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. IV immunoglobulin
  2. High-dose IV corticosteroids
  3. IV acyclovir
  4. Exchange transfusion
Answer: A. This is Guillain-Barré syndrome (cyto-albumin dissociation post-Campylobacter); DOC is IVIG 2 g/kg (or plasmapheresis). Steroids are contraindicated in GBS.

Q4. A 4-month-old boy is floppy with poor head control. He is alert with a normal facial expression, has tongue fasciculations and absent deep tendon reflexes. Creatine kinase is normal. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Spastic cerebral palsy
  2. Down syndrome
  3. Duchenne muscular dystrophy
  4. Spinal muscular atrophy (Werdnig-Hoffman)
Answer: D. Peripheral (LMN) hypotonia with tongue fasciculations, areflexia, preserved alertness and normal CK is classic SMA type I. CP gives UMN signs (hyperreflexia); Duchenne presents later with very high CK.

Q5. A 7-year-old with sickle cell disease presents with sudden right-sided weakness and dysphasia. CT shows no hemorrhage; MRI confirms a left MCA-territory infarct. In addition to supportive measures, which is the key specific intervention?

  1. IV thrombolysis (tPA)
  2. Exchange transfusion
  3. High-dose corticosteroids
  4. Immediate decompressive craniectomy
Answer: B. SCD is the MC cause of pediatric stroke; ischemic stroke due to SCD is treated with exchange transfusion to reduce sickle hemoglobin. Thrombolysis is not recommended by pediatric stroke guidelines.

  • No UTI without a positive culture: suprapubic = any growth, catheter ≥10⁴, clean-catch ≥10⁵ CFU/mL. E. coli ~80%.

  • <6 months UTI more in boys; after 6 months more in girls; up to 50% have a structural abnormality.
  • Casts: RBC → glomerular, WBC → pyelonephritis; nitrite +ve = UTI (−ve doesn't exclude).
  • Tx: cystitis = oral 3–5 d; pyelonephritis = admit + IV; VUR grades I–III prophylaxis, IV–V reimplant. Imaging US → MCUG (VUR) → DMSA (scar).
  • Hypospadias → do NOT circumcise; bilateral renal agenesis = Potter (lethal pulmonary hypoplasia).

Renal failure & Miscellaneous

Test Yourself — Nephrology

  4. Alport syndrome
Answer: B. Nephritic picture (haematuria + HTN) ~1–3 weeks after pharyngitis with low C3, normal C4 = PSGN. IgA would follow URTI within days, not weeks.

  3. Cyclophosphamide

Answer: A. Classic minimal change nephrotic syndrome (age, normal BP/C3) — start steroids (>90% respond); biopsy/second-line agents only if resistant, relapsing or atypical features.

  1. Vesicoureteral reflux
  2. Multicystic dysplastic kidney
  3. Posterior urethral valves
  4. Wilms tumour
Answer: C. Poor stream in a boy = PUV until proven otherwise; bilateral hydronephrosis/hydroureter supports it. Confirm with MCUG/endoscopy; untreated leads to VUR → scarring → CKD.

  1. Chronic kidney disease

  3. Post-renal obstruction
  4. Pre-renal AKI
Answer: D. Avid Na retention (low urine Na, FE Na <1%, high osmolality, BUN:Cr >20:1) = pre-renal AKI from hypovolaemia — treat with a 20 ml/kg fluid bolus. ATN would show FE Na >2%.

Q5. A 2-year-old is found to have a smooth, firm, unilateral abdominal mass that does not cross the midline, with painless haematuria and hypertension. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Neuroblastoma

  3. Wilms tumour

Answer: C. A well-circumscribed mass that does not cross the midline with haematuria and HTN = Wilms (nephroblastoma). Neuroblastoma is irregular, crosses the midline, raises urinary VMA/HVA and lacks haematuria.

6. Hematology

Anemia — framework & lab basics

Definition Classify anemia by size (MCV 80–100 → micro/normo/macrocytic) and by mechanism (low production vs hemolysis vs blood loss).

  • Film: target → thalassemia; sickle → SCD; spherocyte → HS/AIHA; schistocyte → MAHA; Heinz/bite → G6PD; Howell–Jolly → asplenia/SCD; basophilic stippling → lead.

  • Mediterranean child, microcytic with normal/high RBC count, Mentzer <13, target cells, normal ferritin = thalassemia (not IDA).

  • Tx: transfuse to Hb >9.5 + folic acid; chelation (deferoxamine/deferasirox); BMT curative.

Sickle cell disease

Definition AR β-chain defect: valine for glutamic acid at position 6 → Hb S polymerizes under hypoxia → vaso-occlusion + hemolysis.
Red flags Acute chest syndrome (SOB, chest pain, fever, CXR infiltrate) is fatal; sequestration → shock. Parvovirus B19 → aplastic crisis.
MCQ pearls
  • Dactylitis in a toddler = classic first SCD presentation; film shows sickle cells + Howell–Jolly (autosplenectomy).
  • Electrophoresis: ↑Hb S, absent Hb A. Tx: folic acid + hydroxyurea (raises Hb F), prophylactic penicillin to age 5, VOC = hydration/O2/analgesia; SCT curative.
  • Trait (Hb AS) is asymptomatic and protects against malaria.

G6PD deficiency & hereditary spherocytosis

Definition G6PD = X-linked recessive, oxidative hemolysis after triggers; HS = autosomal dominant spectrin/ankyrin defect, extravascular hemolysis.
MCQ pearls
  • Boy + jaundice/dark urine 24–72 h after fava beans or sulfa/nitrofurantoin + bite cells/Heinz bodies = G6PD (assay 2–3 months after attack).
  • Spherocytes + Coombs NEGATIVE = HS (osmotic fragility diagnostic); spherocytes + Coombs POSITIVE = AIHA.
  • HS Tx: folic acid; splenectomy after age 5–6 (vaccinate + lifelong penicillin).

Macrocytic & aplastic anemias

Definition Megaloblastic = B12 (terminal ileum) or folate deficiency; aplastic = pancytopenia + hypoplastic marrow.

  • Macrocytic + hypersegmented neutrophils; only B12 causes neuropathy (subacute combined degeneration). Folate = no neuropathy.

  • Fanconi anemia: commonest inherited aplastic, AR, short stature + absent thumb/radii + café-au-lait, macrocytic, chromosome breakage, high leukemia risk.

Definition Underproduction of GH (alone or with other anterior pituitary hormones = panhypopituitarism); GH is pulsatile so a random level is useless.

MCQ pearls
  • Palpable purpura on the legs + abdominal pain + arthritis after a URI = HSP; check UA for renal involvement.
  • HSP labs: ANA/RF/ANCA negative, normal coagulation, normal-to-high platelets (low platelets argue against HSP).
  • Fever ≥5 days + 4 of 5 CRASH features (Conjunctivitis, Rash, Adenopathy, Strawberry tongue, Hands/feet) = Kawasaki.
  • Kawasaki treatment is IVIG + aspirin (one of the few paediatric aspirin indications) to prevent coronary aneurysms.
  • Fever + only 2–3 criteria + thrombocytosis/low albumin/elevated ALT = think incomplete Kawasaki — still get an echo.

Test Yourself — Rheumatology

Q1. A 3-year-old has refused to walk for 1 day. He is febrile (39°C), holds the right hip in flexion and external rotation, and screams on any movement. CRP and WBC are markedly elevated. Which is the most appropriate next step?

  1. Reassure and review in 1 week
  2. Start oral ibuprofen for transient synovitis
  3. Urgent joint aspiration with Gram stain and culture
  4. Plain X-ray and discharge if normal
Answer: C. Fever, inability to bear weight, and high inflammatory markers indicate septic arthritis — aspirate to confirm and guide antibiotics (hip septic arthritis is a surgical emergency). Transient synovitis (B) would have normal labs and no fever; an early X-ray (D) is often normal and cannot exclude infection.

Q2. A 5-year-old has high spiking daily fevers for 3 weeks, a salmon-pink rash that comes and goes with the fever, hepatosplenomegaly, and arthritis of both knees. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Systemic-onset JIA
  2. Oligoarticular JIA
  3. Reactive arthritis
  4. Henoch-Schonlein purpura
Answer: A. Daily (quotidian) fever, evanescent salmon-pink rash, organomegaly and arthritis are the defining picture of systemic-onset JIA. Oligoarticular JIA (B) is afebrile with ≤4 joints; HSP (D) presents with palpable purpura, not a salmon rash.

Q3. A 6-year-old develops palpable, non-blanching purpura over the buttocks and lower legs, colicky abdominal pain, and painful swollen ankles two weeks after a sore throat. Platelets and coagulation studies are normal. Which test best supports the diagnosis if biopsy is performed?

  1. Low platelet count
  2. Positive ANCA
  3. IgA deposition in small vessels
  4. Positive rheumatoid factor
Answer: C. This is HSP, an IgA-mediated small-vessel vasculitis; biopsy shows IgA deposition (skin leukocytoclastic angiitis, mesangial IgA in kidney). Platelets are normal/high not low (A), and ANCA/RF are negative in HSP (B, D).

Q4. A 2-year-old has had fever for 6 days plus bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, cracked red lips with a strawberry tongue, a truncal rash, and swollen hands. Which intervention most reduces his risk of long-term cardiac complications?

  1. Oral corticosteroids alone
  2. IVIG plus aspirin
  3. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics
  4. Supportive care and NSAIDs only
Answer: B. Fever ≥5 days plus 4 of 5 CRASH criteria = Kawasaki disease; IVIG (2 g/kg) plus aspirin drops the coronary aneurysm rate from ~15–20% to ~2–4%. Antibiotics (C) and supportive care alone (D) do not protect the coronaries.

Q5. A previously well 7-year-old has had a limp for 6 hours after a recent cold. He is afebrile, looks well, and has mildly limited hip internal rotation; CBC and CRP are normal. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Septic arthritis of the hip
  2. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis
  3. Legg-Calve-Perthes disease
  4. Transient synovitis
Answer: D. An afebrile, well-appearing child with a post-viral limp and normal inflammatory markers fits transient synovitis (self-limited, resolves in days). Septic arthritis (A) would be febrile with high CRP; SCFE (B) affects overweight adolescents; Perthes (C) causes a more chronic painless limp with X-ray changes.

Test Yourself — Genetics

Q1. A newborn male has a flat nasal bridge, up-slanting palpebral fissures, a single transverse palmar crease and central hypotonia. Echo shows an endocardial cushion defect. Which is the most likely cardiac lesion?

  1. Ventricular septal defect
  2. Atrioventricular septal defect
  3. Coarctation of the aorta
  4. Tetralogy of Fallot
Answer: B. The picture is Down syndrome, where the MC CHD (40%) is the AVSD / endocardial cushion defect. Coarctation is the Turner association, not Down.

Q2. A growth-restricted neonate has microcephaly, micrognathia, a short sternum, clenched fists with overlapping fingers and rocker-bottom feet. Pregnancy was complicated by polyhydramnios. Which trisomy is most likely?

  1. Trisomy 13 (Patau)
  2. Trisomy 21 (Down)
  3. Trisomy 18 (Edwards)
  4. Triploidy
Answer: C. Clenched fists + rocker-bottom feet + micrognathia + short sternum + polyhydramnios = Edwards (T18). Patau (A) is keyed by polydactyly, cleft palate and holoprosencephaly.

Q3. A 14-year-old girl is investigated for short stature and primary amenorrhoea. She has a webbed neck, widely spaced nipples and a wide carrying angle; LH and FSH are elevated. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Turner syndrome
  2. Klinefelter syndrome
  3. Noonan syndrome
  4. Fragile X syndrome
Answer: A. Short stature (cardinal), webbed neck, ovarian dysgenesis with high LH/FSH in a phenotypic female = Turner (45,XO). Noonan (C) is AD with a normal karyotype and pulmonary stenosis, affecting both sexes.

Q4. A family is affected by a disorder transmitted only through mothers; affected fathers never pass it to any of their children. Which inheritance pattern does this describe?

  1. X-linked recessive
  2. Autosomal dominant
  3. X-linked dominant
  4. Mitochondrial
Answer: D. Maternal-only transmission with no paternal transmission is mitochondrial (only the ovum contributes cytoplasm). In X-linked dominant (C) an affected father transmits to all daughters, so children can be affected through fathers.

Q5. A 4-year-old has an elfin face, an overly friendly personality, a supravalvular aortic stenosis murmur and transient neonatal hypercalcaemia. Which condition is most likely?

  1. DiGeorge syndrome
  2. William's syndrome
  3. Down syndrome
  4. Prader-Willi syndrome
Answer: B. Elfin facies, supravalvular AS, hypercalcaemia and the sociable personality define William's (microdeletion 7). DiGeorge (A) is microdeletion 22 with the opposite electrolyte picture — hypocalcaemia from parathyroid hypoplasia.

Breastfeeding vs Formula & Feeding Rules

Definition Breast milk is ideal sole nutrition for the first 6 months; formula is humanized cow's milk for when breastfeeding is not possible.
Red flag No honey before 1 year (infant botulism); no whole cow's milk, water before 6 mo.

  • Good on breast milk but diarrhea on formula → cow's milk allergy.
  • Acute GE resolves but diarrhea persists with milk → secondary lactose intolerance.
  • Diarrhea + distension after introducing cereal → celiac disease.
  • Vit K IM at birth for all; exclusively breastfed → start vit D 400 IU + iron.

Vitamin D & Rickets

Definition Rickets = failure of mineralization of osteoid in growing bone from vit D deficiency (D3 from skin UV, D2 from plants).
Red flag Excess vit D therapy → hypervitaminosis D: hypercalcemia, anorexia/vomiting, polyuria, renal stones, nephrocalcinosis.
MCQ pearls
  • Craniotabes = first sign; rachitic rosary, Harrison sulcus, genu varum.
  • ALP = most sensitive for activity; 25-OH vit D = most accurate store.
  • Wrist X-ray triad = widening, cupping, fraying.
  • Tx: vit D3 IM 600,000 IU or oral 3000–5000 IU/day; prevention 400 IU/day.
  • Alopecia → VDDR type 2; X-linked hypophosphatemic = renal phosphate wasting.
  • "3 W's" of low sun = wrapping, winter, windows; coexists with IDA.

Vitamin Deficiency Syndromes

Definition Fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) are stored with toxicity risk; water-soluble (B-group, C) deplete fast.
MCQ pearls
  • Night blindness / Bitot spots → vit A.
  • Bleeding newborn → vit K (given IM at birth); scurvy (bleeding gums) → vit C.
  • 3 D's (diarrhea, dermatitis, dementia) → niacin (B3).
  • B1 → beriberi/Wernicke; B6 → infant seizures; B12/folate → megaloblastic anemia (folate → NTDs).

Test Yourself — Nutrition & Growth

Q1. A 9-month-old exclusively breastfed infant, kept indoors and swaddled through winter, has a soft compressible occiput, widened wrists, and beading along the costochondral junctions. Serum ALP is markedly raised. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Osteogenesis imperfecta
  2. Vitamin D deficiency rickets
  3. Scurvy
  4. Achondroplasia
Answer: B. Exclusive breastfeeding (low vit D) + the "3 W's" (wrapping, winter, windows) + craniotabes, rachitic rosary and raised ALP are classic nutritional rickets. Scurvy causes bleeding gums, not these bony mineralization signs.

Q2. A 2-year-old from a region of famine has generalized pitting edema, a "moon" face, a hypopigmented "flag-sign" in his hair, and hepatomegaly. His weight appears near-normal because of the swelling. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Marasmus
  2. Nephrotic syndrome
  3. Kwashiorkor
  4. Congestive heart failure
Answer: C. Edema + fatty liver + flag-sign hair with relatively preserved fat is protein-deficiency kwashiorkor (hypoalbuminemia → ↓ oncotic pressure). Marasmus is calorie deficiency with severe wasting and no edema.

Q3. A healthy term infant weighed 3.2 kg at birth. Assuming normal growth, what is the expected weight at 1 year of age?

  1. ~6.4 kg (double)
  2. ~9.6 kg (triple)
  3. ~12.8 kg (quadruple)
  4. ~4.8 kg
Answer: B. Birth weight triples by 1 year (doubles by 6 months, quadruples by 2 years). 3.2 × 3 ≈ 9.6 kg. Doubling (A) is the 6-month value; quadrupling (C) is the 2-year value.

Q4. A 7-month-old infant, well on breast milk, develops persistent watery diarrhea each time the mother trials a cow's-milk formula, but is symptom-free back on the breast. Which is the most likely cause?

  1. Cow's milk protein allergy
  2. Celiac disease
  3. Galactosemia
  4. Constitutional growth delay
Answer: A. Thriving on breast milk but diarrhea triggered specifically by cow's-milk formula is the sheet's signature of cow's-milk protein allergy. Celiac would appear after introducing wheat/cereal, not formula.

Q5. A toddler treated for rickets has been kept on high-dose oral vitamin D for many months. He now has anorexia, vomiting, constipation, polyuria and polydipsia, and renal ultrasound shows nephrocalcinosis. Which is the most likely explanation?

  1. Persistent vitamin D deficiency
  2. Hypocalcemic tetany
  3. Vitamin D toxicity (hypervitaminosis D)
  4. Familial hypophosphatemic rickets
Answer: C. Prolonged vitamin D therapy → hypervitaminosis D: hypercalcemia with GI upset, polyuria/polydipsia, renal stones and nephrocalcinosis. Manage by stopping vit D and Ca, IV fluids, and steroids. Deficiency (A) would cause hypocalcemia, not hypercalcemia.

Q1. A 9-month-old is brought for a check. He sits unsupported and crawls, but cries and clings when the doctor approaches and his mother steps back. He says “baba” without clearly meaning his father. Which finding is most appropriate for his age?

  1. Mature pincer grasp
  2. Separation anxiety
  3. Two-word sentences

Q2. A 7-month-old, ex-term, has a persistent palmar grasp and Moro reflex, increased lower-limb tone with scissoring, and cannot sit. Which is the most likely explanation?

  1. Normal variation
  2. Benign delayed maturation
  3. Cerebral palsy
  4. Werdnig-Hoffmann (SMA)
Answer: C. Primitive reflexes should be gone by 3–6 m; their persistence with hypertonia and scissoring is the classic picture of cerebral palsy. SMA causes hypotonia, not scissoring.

Q3. A 4-month-old on chemotherapy for leukaemia is due his routine vaccines. Which scheduled vaccine is contraindicated?

  1. IPV (in the Hexa vaccine)
  2. Hib (in the Hexa vaccine)
  3. Hepatitis B
  4. Oral polio vaccine (OPV)
Answer: D. OPV is a live-attenuated vaccine, contraindicated in immunocompromise. IPV, Hib (killed) and HBV (recombinant) are safe.

Q4. A neonate born to an HBsAg-positive mother is delivered vaginally. What is the most appropriate immediate intervention?

  1. Hepatitis B vaccine + HBIG within 12 hours
  2. Hepatitis B vaccine alone at 2 months
  3. Lamivudine prophylaxis only
  4. No action; check serology at 9 months
Answer: A. Babies of HBsAg+ mothers need both active (vaccine) and passive (HBIG 0.5 mL) immunisation within 12 hours of birth.

Q5. A 2-month-old received his first DTaP-containing vaccine and developed an encephalopathy within 5 days, not attributable to another cause. Regarding future doses, the correct stance is:

  1. Give the next DTaP normally
  2. The pertussis component is an absolute contraindication to further DTaP
  3. Switch to oral polio vaccine instead
  4. Give DTaP but premedicate with paracetamol
Answer: B. Encephalopathy within 7 days of a prior dose, with no other cause, is an absolute contraindication to further DTaP. OPV is unrelated and does not substitute for DTaP.
