1. Respiratory

Pediatric respiratory disease splits into airway problems (upper = stridor/croup/epiglottitis; lower = bronchiolitis, asthma, foreign body) versus parenchymal and pleural problems (pneumonia, cystic fibrosis, effusion) — and because viruses cause 80–90% of childhood respiratory infections, infectious causes lead almost every presentation.

Introduction: Respiratory System & Respiratory Infections

Definition
  • Upper airway (above thoracic inlet): narrows on inspiration, dilates on expiration → extrathoracic obstruction is worse on inspiration (stridor).
  • Lower airway (below thoracic inlet): dilates on inspiration, narrows on expiration → intrathoracic obstruction is worse on expiration (wheeze).
  • Right lung = 3 lobes (55% of total lung volume); left = 2 lobes.
Why Breathlessness = increased work of breathing from airway obstruction (asthma, bronchiolitis, FB, croup), parenchymal disease (pneumonia), pleural disease (pneumothorax), or CVS (heart failure) — the four buckets that organise this whole system. Newborns are obligate nasal breathers; distress that improves with crying = choanal atresia.

Upper respiratory tract infections
  • Common cold (coryza): commonest infection of childhood; rhinovirus (MC), coronavirus, RSV. Self-limiting (5–7 days); no role for antibiotics/antitussives. MC complication = otitis media (5%).
  • Pharyngitis/tonsillitis: viral = adenovirus (MC); bacterial = Group A beta-haemolytic strep (S. pyogenes), rare <3 y. Petechiae on soft palate suggests strep. Penicillin × 10 days — main benefit is preventing acute rheumatic fever (start within 9 days).
  • Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy): MC deep neck infection, S. pyogenes, >8 y → "hot potato" voice, trismus, uvula displaced to contralateral side.
  • Retropharyngeal abscess: <6 y → high fever, drooling, extended neck to breathe; thickened posterior pharyngeal wall on imaging.
  • Sinusitis: suspect if cold ≥10 days or worsening; bacterial = S. pneumoniae (MC), non-typeable H. influenzae. CT = gold standard (X-ray NOT helpful). Tx: high-dose amoxicillin 90 mg/kg/day.
  • Acute otitis media: peak 6–24 months; bacterial MC = S. pneumoniae (40%). 80% resolve spontaneously; high-dose amoxicillin if severe. Glue ear = MC cause of conductive hearing loss.
MCQ pearls
  • Respiratory disorders = 50% of all illness <5 y; viruses cause 80–90% of childhood respiratory infections.
  • Asthma = most common chronic illness of childhood.
  • Tongue depressor / throat exam is contraindicated when upper-airway obstruction is suspected.
  • Tonsillectomy criteria: 7 episodes/1 y, 5/y × 2 y, or 3/y × 3 y.

Pneumonia

Definition Inflammation of the lung parenchyma, mostly from aspiration of airborne pathogens. CAP = not acquired in hospital; HAP = onset ≥48–72 h after admission.
Why Alveolar exudate causes consolidation → dullness, bronchial breathing, increased vocal resonance/fremitus. Lobar (homogenous, one lobe) is usually bacterial; interstitial (bilateral perihilar) is usually viral.
Presentation
  • Fever, malaise, poor feeding, vomiting, abdominal pain; neck pain = pleural irritation.
  • Cough (dry → productive), breathlessness, grunting.
  • Tachypnea = most sensitive clinical sign; classical consolidation signs often absent in young children.

Management
  • Most managed at home. Admit if: age <3–6 mo, SpO₂ <92%, severe distress/grunting, recurrent apnea, dehydration, complications, or no response to oral Rx.
  • Antibiotics by age: Newborn = IV ampicillin + gentamicin (or 3rd-gen cephalosporin); infant/young child = amoxicillin/co-amoxiclav; >5 y = amoxicillin or a macrolide.
  • Critically ill/immunocompromised → 3rd-gen cephalosporin + vancomycin. Physiotherapy has no proven role (except CF/bronchiectasis).
Red flags / complications
  • Persistent fever or worsening distress despite appropriate Rx → suspect complication.
  • Pneumatocele (air cyst, post-S. aureus, resolves), lung abscess (S. aureus + Klebsiella), parapneumonic effusion, empyema (purulent → needs chest tube drainage), pneumothorax.
  • Recurrent pneumonia = ≥2/year or ≥3 ever with full resolution between. Same site → FBA or congenital malformation; different sites → immunodeficiency, CF, CHD (VSD), GERD.
MCQ pearls — atypical/special types
  • Chlamydia trachomatis (1–3 mo): afebrile + conjunctivitis + staccato cough + eosinophilia → macrolide.
  • Mycoplasma (5–15 y): "walking pneumonia", insidious, CXR worse than clinical picture; cold agglutinins (IgM); extrapulmonary: erythema multiforme/SJS, AIHA, Guillain-Barré → macrolide.
  • S. aureus (<1 y): sudden, toxic, high fever, pneumatocele + empyema → cloxacillin/vancomycin.
  • Aspiration pneumonia: anaerobes, right upper/apical lobe; kerosene/hydrocarbon ingestion → NO gastric lavage (supportive only).
  • WHO ARI: high RR alone = pneumonia (oral abx home); + lower chest indrawing = severe (refer); + danger signs = very severe.

Stridor (Croup, Epiglottitis, Bacterial Tracheitis & others)

Definition Harsh, monophonic, high-pitched inspiratory noise from upper (extrathoracic) airway partial obstruction. It is an emergency — the priority is always a patent airway.
Why On inspiration the extrathoracic airway becomes more negative downstream of the obstruction → further collapse → inspiratory stridor.
Classification
  • By phase: inspiratory = extrathoracic (croup, epiglottitis, laryngomalacia); expiratory = intrathoracic (bacterial tracheitis, tracheal stenosis, anaphylaxis); biphasic = fixed glottic/subglottic (subglottic stenosis, hemangioma).
  • Acute: croup (MC), epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, FBA, angioedema.
  • Chronic/congenital: laryngomalacia (MC), vocal cord palsy, vascular ring, subglottic stenosis, laryngeal web.

Croup management (by severity)
  • Mild (barking cough, no stridor at rest): home care, humidified air, hydration, reassurance.
  • Moderate: humidified O₂ + dexamethasone (oral/IM) or nebulised budesonide.
  • Severe (stridor at rest, low SpO₂): admit, O₂, steroid + nebulised epinephrine (watch for rebound — observe 3–4 h), rarely intubation. Admit if <12 months (narrow airway).
  • Recovery in 2–5 days; complication = viral pneumonia (1–2%); no parainfluenza vaccine.
Red flags Drooling, cyanosis, decreased air entry, suprasternal/intercostal retractions, silent chest (late), lethargy. In suspected obstruction: do NOT examine the throat, do NOT lie flat, do NOT delay for X-ray (steeple sign) — keep the child calm on the parent's lap.
MCQ pearls
  • Steeple sign (subglottic narrowing) = croup; thumb sign (lateral neck) = epiglottitis ("not done" — diagnose in OR); cherry-red epiglottis on direct laryngoscopy.
  • Laryngomalacia: MC chronic/congenital stridor; inspiratory, worse supine/crying, thriving with normal SpO₂; omega-shaped epiglottis on laryngoscopy; self-limits by 18–24 months.
  • Subglottic stenosis = biphasic stridor; bronchoscopy is gold standard.
  • Epiglottitis prevention = HiB vaccine + rifampicin prophylaxis to contacts. Steroids/adrenaline are ineffective in epiglottitis.
  • Bacterial tracheitis = fails to improve with croup therapy + toxic child.

Acute Bronchiolitis & Foreign Body Aspiration

Definition Bronchiolitis = acute inflammation of the bronchioles; the commonest serious respiratory infection of infancy. FBA = inhalation of an object into the lower airways.
Why (bronchiolitis) Viral invasion → mucosal oedema + exudate → bilateral bronchiolar narrowing → air trapping and hyperinflation (hence wheeze, crackles, and a liver pushed down by hyperinflated lungs).
Presentation — Bronchiolitis
  • RSV (70–80%, MC); age <2 y, peak 3–6 months; winter/early spring. Not diagnosed after age 2.
  • URTI prodrome (low-grade fever, coryza) → cough, wheeze, fast breathing, poor feeding; apnea may be the first sign in young infants.
  • Exam: bilateral fine crackles + wheeze/rhonchi, prolonged expiration, retractions; no stridor. CXR: hyperinflation (lucency, flat diaphragms).

Management — Bronchiolitis
  • Mainly supportive: hydration (IVF/NGT), humidified O₂, monitoring; CPAP/ventilation if severe.
  • No proven role for corticosteroids, antihistamines, physiotherapy, or hypertonic saline. Antibiotics only for secondary bacterial infection. Ribavirin only in high-risk infants.
  • Admit if: high-risk (preterm/VLBW <1.5 kg, CHD, chronic lung disease, immunodeficiency), SpO₂ <92%, RR 60–70, grunting/cyanosis, apnea, poor feeding/dehydration, age <3–6 mo.
  • Prevention: hand-washing + Palivizumab (monthly IM, high-risk <2 y). No RSV vaccine.
Presentation — FBA
  • Common site = right main bronchus (shorter, wider, more vertical); MC object = organic/nuts.
  • Acute: choking, paroxysmal cough, gagging, wheeze, stridor (if laryngeal). Chronic (missed): recurrent cough, unilateral wheeze, recurrent pneumonia.
  • Signs: unilateral decreased/absent air entry, rhonchi, choking.
Diagnosis — FBA
  • Most FBs are radiolucent → CXR (expiration view) for indirect signs: partial obstruction = ipsilateral air-trapping/hyperinflation (ball-valve); complete = ipsilateral collapse + mediastinal shift toward affected side.
  • Rigid bronchoscopy = gold standard, diagnostic + therapeutic (definitive removal).
Management — FBA (first aid)
  • <1 year: 5 back blows (prone, head down) → 5 chest thrusts (no abdominal thrusts).
  • >1 year: Heimlich (abdominal thrusts) if conscious; CPR + abdominal thrusts if unconscious.
  • If stable & no distress → rigid bronchoscopy for removal.
MCQ pearls
  • Bronchiolitis complications: apnea, dehydration, pneumothorax, bronchiolitis obliterans (adenovirus 3,7,21), increased asthma risk.
  • Best way to separate bronchiolitis from pneumonia = clinical + radiological.
  • Infant under 1 with FBA = no abdominal thrusts (risk of organ injury).

Asthma

Definition Chronic disease with reversible airway obstruction (spontaneous or with drugs), increased bronchial reactivity, and airway inflammation.
Why Atopic, polygenic, IgE-mediated inflammation → bronchoconstriction triggered by URTIs, allergens (house dust mite), smoke, cold air, exercise, emotion. Reversibility is what distinguishes it.
Presentation
  • Triad of recurrent attacks: cough (nocturnal, post-exertion, cold air; may be the only symptom = cough-variant asthma), wheeze, and SOB; asymptomatic between attacks.
  • Compatible: recurrent attacks, +ve family atopy, age >2 y, relief by bronchodilator, worse with triggers, nocturnal symptoms.
  • Chronic signs: barrel chest, Harrison sulcus, bilateral ↓expansion, prolonged expiration, bilateral rhonchi.
Diagnosis
  • Clinical diagnosis. Support: eosinophilia, ↑IgE; PEF (>5 y; diurnal variability >20% supports asthma); spirometry obstructive (↓FEV₁/FVC <80%) with reversibility (FEV₁ ↑>12% post-bronchodilator); skin-prick test for atopy.
  • CXR only if failed treatment, suspected pneumonia/pneumothorax, or sudden deterioration.

Management
  • SABA (salbutamol, terbutaline) = rescue, onset 10–15 min; S/E tachycardia, tremor, hypokalaemia.
  • Inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) = "preventer", the best controller and most important maintenance drug. LABA (salmeterol) = 1st add-on >5 y, never alone, useful in exercise-induced asthma.
  • Others: ipratropium (synergistic in acute attacks), LTRA, theophylline (narrow index), omalizumab (anti-IgE).
  • Acute exacerbation: ABC + O₂ → nebulised salbutamol (up to ×3) + oral/IV steroid → escalate: ipratropium, IV salbutamol, IV magnesium sulfate, aminophylline → refractory: ketamine, mechanical ventilation. MC cause of poor control = non-adherence.
  • Delivery: nebuliser (emergency, all ages); MDI/dry powder >5 y; MDI + spacer = any age (≈nebuliser).
Red flags — severe/life-threatening
  • Severe: talks in words, tripod, accessory muscles, HR >120, RR >30, PEFR 40–69%, SpO₂ 90–95%.
  • Life-threatening: silent chest, paradoxical breathing, bradycardia, altered mental status, PEFR <40%, SpO₂ <90%, hypercapnia (PaCO₂ ≥42).
  • Admit if: no clinical response, exhausted, FEV₁/PEF <50% predicted, SpO₂ <92%.
MCQ pearls
  • Asthma = 10% of children in Jordan; commonest chronic respiratory disease of childhood; MC cause of school absence; ages 2–10, M>F.
  • Preschool wheeze: viral episodic (URTI-triggered, resolves by 5 y) vs multiple-trigger (atopy, more likely true asthma).
  • A normalising PaCO₂ in a tiring asthmatic = ominous, not reassuring.

Cystic Fibrosis & Pleural Effusion

Definition CF = autosomal recessive defect in the CFTR gene (chromosome 7); ΔF508 deletion is MC (75%). Common in Caucasians. Pleural effusion = fluid in the pleural cavity.
Why Defective CFTR impairs chloride/sodium transport → thick viscous secretions in lungs, pancreas, gut, liver → a multisystem disease.
Presentation — CF
  • Neonate: meconium ileus (10–20%) — fails to pass meconium in first 48 h; prolonged jaundice; salty taste when kissed; pseudo-Bartter (↓K/Cl, metabolic alkalosis).
  • Respiratory: chronic cough (MC presentation), recurrent wheezy chest, nasal polyps/pansinusitis, bronchiectasis. Pathogens: early = S. aureus (MC), H. influenzae; late = Pseudomonas (most important), Burkholderia cepacia.
  • GIT: pancreatic insufficiency → steatorrhea + FTT; CF-related diabetes (old child); rectal prolapse; cirrhosis/portal HTN. Males: infertility (absent vas deferens); clubbing.
Diagnosis — CF
  • Screening: ↑ immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT) initial; CFTR DNA testing.
  • Sweat test (pilocarpine iontophoresis) = diagnostic: positive if Cl⁻ >60 mmol/L; repeat 2–3×. Genetic testing confirms (or if borderline Cl 30–59).
Management — CF (multidisciplinary)
  • Chest physiotherapy ≥twice daily; bronchodilators; mucolytics (DNase/acetylcysteine 2 h before physio).
  • Antibiotics (oral when well, IV cloxacillin/tobramycin for exacerbations, nebulised tobramycin); azithromycin long-term to prevent infections.
  • Nutrition: pancreatic enzyme replacement, fat-soluble vitamins, high-calorie diet (target BMI ≥50th centile). CFTR modulators (gene therapy) for ΔF508. Insulin for CF-related DM.
  • Vaccinate (flu, pneumococcal); Palivizumab <24 m. Prognosis: survival ~35 y; 95% die of respiratory failure (→ lung transplant).

Test Yourself — Respiratory

Q1. A 4-year-old boy has a 2-day coryzal prodrome followed by a barking cough, hoarse voice, and inspiratory stridor worse at night. He is mildly unwell, afebrile-to-low-grade, and drinking normally. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Acute epiglottitis
  2. Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis)
  3. Bacterial tracheitis
  4. Foreign body aspiration
Answer: B. Gradual onset, coryza prodrome, barking cough, hoarse voice, and ability to drink = viral croup (parainfluenza). Epiglottitis (A) is sudden, toxic, drooling, and unable to drink with a muffled voice.

Q2. A 6-month-old presents in winter with 2 days of coryza then cough, wheeze, poor feeding and fast breathing. Exam: bilateral fine crackles and wheeze, retractions, SpO₂ 94%, no stridor. There is no family history of atopy. Which statement about management is correct?

  1. Nebulised corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment
  2. Oral antibiotics should be started empirically
  3. Management is mainly supportive (hydration, oxygen, monitoring)
  4. A chest X-ray is mandatory to confirm the diagnosis
Answer: C. This is RSV bronchiolitis (<2 y, winter, 1st wheeze, no atopy) — diagnosed clinically and treated supportively. Steroids (A), antibiotics (B), and routine CXR (D) have no proven role.

Q3. A previously well 18-month-old suddenly develops choking and paroxysmal coughing while eating nuts. He stabilises but has reduced air entry over the right lung. Expiratory chest X-ray shows hyperinflation of the right lung. What is the most appropriate definitive step?

  1. Rigid bronchoscopy
  2. A course of oral amoxicillin
  3. Nebulised salbutamol and discharge
  4. CT chest then observation
Answer: A. Choking on nuts + unilateral signs + ipsilateral air-trapping (ball-valve) = foreign body aspiration; rigid bronchoscopy is both diagnostic and the definitive therapeutic removal. Antibiotics/bronchodilators (B, C) do not address the obstruction.

Q4. A 2-month-old afebrile infant has tachypnea, a staccato cough, and conjunctivitis. Bloods show eosinophilia. The mother had an untreated genital infection. Which is the most likely organism and treatment?

  1. Group B streptococcus — IV ampicillin + gentamicin
  2. Respiratory syncytial virus — supportive care
  3. Chlamydia trachomatis — a macrolide
  4. Staphylococcus aureus — cloxacillin
Answer: C. Afebrile pneumonia at 1–3 months with staccato cough, conjunctivitis, and eosinophilia is classic for C. trachomatis → macrolide. GBS (A) causes toxic neonatal pneumonia; S. aureus (D) is sudden, high-fever and toxic with pneumatoceles.

Q5. A 14-year-old with known asthma is brought in with a severe attack. After initial bronchodilators he becomes drowsy with a silent chest. An ABG shows a "normalising" PaCO₂ after being low earlier. What does this indicate?

  1. The attack is resolving and he can be discharged
  2. Impending respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation
  3. A simple respiratory alkalosis
  4. Mild exacerbation needing only continued SABA
Answer: B. A silent chest plus a rising/normalising PaCO₂ in a tiring asthmatic signals impending respiratory failure — ventilate. Early asthma gives respiratory alkalosis (C); the loss of that low CO₂ is the danger sign, not reassurance (A, D).

5. Cardiology

Congenital heart disease (CHD, ~8/1000 live births) splits by colour: acyanotic lesions are left-to-right shunts (VSD, ASD, PDA, AVSD) or obstructions that flood the lungs but stay pink, while cyanotic lesions are right-to-left shunts (the 5 Ts) that bypass the lungs so deoxygenated blood reaches the body — this intro frames the murmurs, S2, and CXR clues that the next topics dissect, then closes with acquired disease (rheumatic fever, IE, HF, arrhythmia).

Introduction to CHD — framework & murmurs

Definition CHD = most common single group of structural malformation in infants, incidence 8/1000 live births; ~30% need intervention in the 1st year. Classified by cyanosis (the master split below).
Why At birth lung expansion drops pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) → LA pressure rises above RA → foramen ovale closes; rising O2 + falling prostaglandins close the ductus (functional 10–15 h, anatomical 2–3 wk). Pressure is higher on the left, so septal defects shunt L→R (pink); only when PVR rises or a lesion bypasses the lungs does flow reverse R→L (blue).

MCQ pearls
  • Most common CHD overall = VSD (~30%).
  • Most common cyanotic CHD in children = TOF; in neonates = TGA.
  • Maternal diabetes → TGA, truncus, tricuspid atresia, VSD. Rubella → PDA + PS. Lithium → Ebstein. SLE → complete heart block.
  • Down (T21) → endocardial cushion / AVSD. Turner → coarctation + bicuspid aortic. Williams → supravalvular AS. Noonan → pulmonary stenosis. DiGeorge → interrupted arch/TOF. Marfan → MVP/aortic dissection.

Acyanotic CHD — L→R shunts & obstruction

Definition Lesions that shunt oxygenated blood left→right (VSD, ASD, PDA, AVSD) or obstruct outflow (AS, PS, coarctation). Child stays pink; the danger is pulmonary over-circulation → HF, FTT, and eventually Eisenmenger (irreversible shunt reversal).
Why Left-sided pressure > right, so blood crosses into the lungs. A large chronic L→R shunt raises PVR until it exceeds systemic pressure → the shunt reverses to R→L = Eisenmenger (cyanosis, clubbing, polycythemia, 2nd–3rd decade) — prevent by early surgery.

AVSD (endocardial cushion defect) Strongly linked to Down syndrome — routine echo in every Down baby. Partial = primum ASD + abnormal AV valve (MR), presents like ASD primum. Complete = "blue and breathless": common single 5-leaflet valve + inlet VSD → HF/FTT at 2–3 wk, cyanosis at birth, usually no murmur. Rx = treat HF + surgery 3–6 mo.
Coarctation of aorta (COA) Localized narrowing, classically just distal to the left subclavian (78%); more in males; Turner association (+ bicuspid aortic). Key sign: upper-limb BP > lower-limb BP (normally lower > upper) with radio-femoral delay. Severe infant type: collapse on day 2 when PDA closes, weak/absent femoral pulse, metabolic acidosis — prostaglandin + surgery. Adult type: hypertension, ejection systolic murmur upper sternal edge, scapular collateral murmur.
COA imaging CXR: rib notching (>6 yr, collaterals) and "figure 3" sign; ECG: LVH; Echo + CT angiography diagnostic. If suspected, measure BP in all 4 limbs.
Red flags
  • Loud S2 in a large VSD = developing pulmonary HTN → Eisenmenger; murmur may soften/disappear as the defect equalizes.
  • Collapsing neonate on day 2 with absent femoral pulses = duct-dependent COA → start PGE1 immediately.
  • Eisenmenger contraindicates closure — only Rx is pulmonary vasodilators (inhaled NO, sildenafil) or heart-lung transplant.
MCQ pearls
  • Fixed wide split S2 → ASD. Continuous machinery murmur + bounding pulse → PDA. Pansystolic at LLSE → VSD.
  • IE prophylaxis applies to VSD/PDA/COA but NOT secundum ASD.
  • PDA in a preterm is not classed as CHD; close it with indomethacin (anti-prostaglandin).
  • Eisenmenger CXR = cardiomegaly + oligemic lung; ECG = RVH + tall (P pulmonale) P wave; CBC = polycythemia.

Why Either blood bypasses the lungs (TGA: parallel circuits) or is forced past an obstruction (TOF: pulmonary stenosis pushes the VSD shunt right-to-left). Many are duct-dependent — survival hinges on keeping the ductus open with PGE1.

Miscellaneous — rheumatic fever, IE, HF, arrhythmia

Heart failure Inability to maintain cardiac output for the body's needs. Signs 3T + 1C: Tachycardia, Tachypnea, Tender enlarged liver, Cardiomegaly on CXR (+ gallop S3, cool peripheries, crackles). Cause by age: neonate = obstruction (severe COA, critical AS, HLHS); infant = large L→R shunt (VSD/PDA/AVSD); older child = RHD, Kawasaki, IE, cardiomyopathy, myocarditis.
HF management Semi-sitting, O2 + pulse oximetry; drugs — diuretic (furosemide) reduces preload, hydralazine reduces afterload, ACEI both, digoxin/dobutamine raises contractility; high-calorie feeds (NGT if tachypneic); treat the cause. Note: ankle edema, orthopnea, PND and congested neck veins are NOT reliable HF signs in infancy.
Rheumatic fever Multisystem autoimmune disease 2–3 wk after group-A strep (GAS) pharyngitis (molecular mimicry), age 5–15 yr, females, developing-world/low socioeconomic. "Licks the joints (no fibrosis), bites the heart (fibrosis)."
Diagnosis — JONES + recent strep Evidence of strep (ASO >300, throat culture) + ≥2 major (or 1 major + 2 minor). Major J♥NES: Joint migratory polyarthritis (large joints), ♥ carditis (pancarditis), subcutaneous Nodules, Erythema marginatum, Sydenham chorea. Minor CAFE: CRP/arthralgia, Fever, ECG changes (+ raised ESR).
RF features Polyarthritis (75%, commonest): migratory, large joints, non-destructive, dramatic salicylate response. Carditis (50%, most serious): pancarditis; endocarditis hits mitral > aortic, left > right (acute MR → chronic MS). Chorea (10%): late, females, may be the sole feature. Erythema marginatum (<5%): pale-centred macules on trunk. Subcutaneous nodules = severe carditis marker.
RF management Salicylates for arthritis/mild carditis (4–6 wk); prednisolone for severe carditis with cardiomegaly; penicillin to eradicate strep; treat HF. Secondary prophylaxis = IM benzathine penicillin: no carditis 5 yr/until 21; carditis 10 yr/until 21; carditis + valve disease 10 yr/until 40; severe carditis = life-long.
Infective endocarditis Infection of the endocardial surface. Substrate = structural heart disease (all CHD except secundum ASD, prosthetic valve, prior IE, MVP+MR) + bacteremia. Commonest organism Strep. viridans; also Staph. aureus/epidermidis, enterococci, HACEK.
IE clues Fever + new/changed murmur, splinter hemorrhages, clubbing, Osler nodules (painful), Janeway lesions (painless), Roth spots, splenomegaly, microscopic hematuria/GN. Always suspect IE with a new murmur + unexplained fever or bacteremia. Dx by Duke criteria (echo vegetations + sustained positive blood cultures — take ≥3 sets before antibiotics). Rx: IV vancomycin + gentamicin (or ceftriaxone) for 6 weeks. Prophylaxis for high-risk only; not for native MVP/MS or routine GI/GU procedures.
SVT & long QT SVT = commonest childhood arrhythmia, HR 250–300, narrow QRS, reentry. Rx: unstable → synchronized DC cardioversion then IV adenosine; stable → vagal manoeuvres (ice to face in infants, Valsalva/carotid massage in children) then adenosine (DOC). Test Yourself — Cardiology

Q1. A 4-year-old is referred for a murmur found at a routine check. He is thriving and asymptomatic. Auscultation reveals a fixed, widely split second heart sound with a soft ejection systolic murmur at the upper left sternal edge. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Ventricular septal defect
  2. Atrial septal defect
  3. Patent ductus arteriosus
  4. Tetralogy of Fallot
Answer: B. A fixed wide split S2 is pathognomonic for ASD (the defect itself is silent; the murmur is from increased pulmonary flow). VSD gives a pansystolic murmur at the LLSE and PDA a continuous machinery murmur; TOF would be cyanotic.

Q2. A 3-day-old term neonate collapses with poor perfusion, weak femoral pulses, and severe metabolic acidosis just as the ductus arteriosus is closing. Blood pressure is markedly higher in the arms than the legs. What is the most appropriate immediate management?

  1. Start a prostaglandin E1 infusion
  2. Give intravenous indomethacin
  3. Give intravenous adenosine
  4. Arrange immediate device closure
Answer: A. This is severe (duct-dependent) coarctation — upper-limb BP > lower-limb — presenting at duct closure. PGE1 reopens the duct to restore lower-body perfusion before surgery. Indomethacin would close the duct (lethal here); adenosine treats SVT.

Q3. A 5-month-old with known cyanotic heart disease becomes acutely blue and breathless while crying, then squats into a knee-chest position; his previously loud systolic murmur becomes quieter. Which lesion best explains this episode?

  1. Transposition of the great arteries
  2. Large ventricular septal defect
  3. Tetralogy of Fallot
  4. Total anomalous pulmonary venous return
Answer: C. A tet spell in TOF: infundibular spasm increases R→L shunt, so cyanosis worsens and the pulmonary-stenosis murmur softens; squatting raises SVR and reduces the shunt. TGA presents with day-1 cyanosis and no murmur.

Q4. A 10-year-old girl presents with migratory large-joint arthritis, a new pansystolic murmur radiating to the axilla, and a raised ASO titre three weeks after a sore throat. Which is the single most important long-term intervention?

  1. Lifelong aspirin
  2. Annual echocardiography only
  3. Regular IM benzathine penicillin prophylaxis
  4. Mitral valve replacement now
Answer: C. This is rheumatic fever with carditis (JONES + recent GAS, mitral involvement). Secondary prophylaxis with IM benzathine penicillin prevents recurrent attacks and progressive valve damage. Aspirin treats acute arthritis only; surgery is not indicated acutely.

Q5. A cyanotic neonate has oxygen saturations that fail to rise after 10 minutes in 100% oxygen. The chest X-ray shows a boot-shaped heart with oligemic lung fields. Which finding fits this diagnosis?

  1. Continuous machinery murmur
  2. Fixed wide split S2
  3. Bounding pulses with wide pulse pressure
  4. Single S2 with a loud ejection systolic murmur
Answer: D. A positive hyperoxia test confirms cyanotic CHD, and a boot-shaped, oligemic CXR is classic TOF, which gives a single S2 (absent pulmonary component) and a loud ESM from the pulmonary stenosis. The other options point to PDA, ASD, and PDA respectively.

2. Gastrointestinal

Covers fluid balance, diarrhea (acute & chronic), oral/esophageal disorders, stomach & duodenum, constipation & the acute abdomen, and liver disease. Dehydration & fluids comes first because rehydration is the mainstay of diarrhea management — the deficit, maintenance and ongoing-loss calculations underpin every diarrheal vignette.

Dehydration & Fluids (foundational)

Definition Net loss of body water/electrolytes; diarrhea (AGE) is the most common cause in children. Severity is graded by % loss of body weight.
Why Infants dehydrate fast: high body-water content, high metabolic rate, large surface-area-to-weight ratio (high insensible loss 15–17 mL/kg/day), immature renal tubular reabsorption, and cannot ask for fluids.
Presentation
  • Mild (~5%): thirst, concentrated urine; no clinical dehydration.
  • Moderate (~10%): irritable, oliguria, tachycardia.
  • Severe (~15%): hypotensive, anuria, lethargic — shock.
  • Hypertonic (Na >150): doughy abdomen, irritability, convulsions, hyperreflexia — intracellular, hard to detect, most serious.

Management — ORS
  • Key component of GE management; powder diluted with water, used within 24h. Not bacteriostatic, not probiotic. Used in all types/ages.
  • New WHO formula: osmolarity 245 mOsm/L, Na 75, glucose 75 mg/dl, Cl 65, K 10, citrate 10, HCO3 20.

  • Coca-Cola & apple juice = low Na, high osmolarity → unsuitable as ORS.
Management — IV fluids
  • Indications: severe dehydration, shock, persistent vomiting, hypertonic dehydration, can't take orally.
  • Maintenance (Holliday-Segar): 1st 10 kg ×100 + 2nd 10 kg ×50 + rest ×20 mL/day. e.g. 23 kg → 1000+500+60 = 1560 mL/day.
  • Deficit = weight × % dehydration × 10. e.g. 9 kg, moderate (10%) → 900 mL. Shocked → ×100; not shocked → ×50.
  • Ongoing loss = weight × number of motions × 10.
  • Isotonic: correct in 24h — Day 1 = maintenance + deficit. Hypertonic: correct over 48–72h, give ½ deficit per day; reduce Na ≤0.5 mmol/L/h (≤12 mmol/L/day) to avoid cerebral edema.
  • Type: 0.9% or 0.45% NaCl + 5% dextrose. Electrolytes: Na 3 mEq/100cc maintenance + 8 mEq/100cc deficit; K 2 mEq/100cc maintenance.
Hypovolemic shock Emergency → ABC, O2, IV/IO access. Bolus 20 mL/kg over 20 min of 0.9% NaCl (or Ringer's lactate); may repeat ×2 then ICU + inotropes. A bolus drops dehydration by ~2%.
Red flags Acidotic (Kussmaul) breathing, bradycardia/hypotension, confusion, blue peripheries, absent urine = decompensated shock. Rapid correction of hypertonic dehydration → cerebral edema/seizures.
Mnemonic: "100-50-20" for daily maintenance; "weight × %dehydration × 10" for deficit.
MCQ pearls
  • Severe dehydration / shock / persistent vomiting → IV, not ORS.
  • Hypernatremic (doughy abdomen + seizures) → correct slowly over 48–72h; never drop Na >12 mmol/L/day.
  • Shock bolus = 20 mL/kg over 20 min; degree of dehydration = (previous wt − current wt) / 10.

Chronic Diarrhea (>14 days)

Definition Diarrhea >14 days; think malabsorption (celiac, giardiasis, CMPI, CHO intolerance, CF), IBD, or toddler's diarrhea.
Presentation — key entities
  • Celiac: 9–18m, 2–3m after gluten intro; steatorrhea (bulky/pale/offensive), distension, FTT, buttock muscle wasting. HLA-DQ2 in 90–95%.

  • CMPI: commonest food allergy of infancy, onset <6m after formula intro; 80% resolve by 1y. ~50% also react to soy → don't use soy formula.

Oral & Esophageal Disorders

Definition Spans congenital clefts, oral infections, GERD, esophageal atresia/TEF, achalasia and caustic ingestion.
Presentation — high-yield
  • Cleft lip/palate: multifactorial; lip repair in 1st 3 months, palate at 10–12 months.
  • Oral thrush: white plaques not removed by depressor → topical nystatin/miconazole; if persistent in older child suspect immunodeficiency.
  • GERD: MC esophageal disorder; infant peak 1–4m, resolves by 12–24m. Postprandial non-projectile, non-bilious vomiting; LES immaturity is MC cause.
  • Esophageal atresia + TEF (85%): polyhydramnios, frothy salivation, choking/cyanosis on first feed.
  • Achalasia: dysphagia more to fluids, halitosis; barium "bird-beak."
Diagnosis
  • GERD: clinical; 24h pH monitoring = best test (pH <4 most of the time); endoscopy for esophagitis.
  • EA/TEF: inability to pass NGT → coiled tube in blind pouch; gas in abdomen = distal fistula present. Screen for VACTERL.
  • Achalasia: manometry most sensitive.
Management
  • GERD: conservative first — 45° semi-sitting, small frequent thickened feeds; then PPI (omeprazole, 1st-line, most potent) / H2 antagonist; domperidone prokinetic. Metoclopramide contraindicated (central extrapyramidal SE). Nissen fundoplication if refractory.
  • Caustic ingestion: ABC, NPO, endoscopy within 24h. No vomiting/lavage/neutralization/charcoal.
Red flags GERD complications: esophagitis → IDA/stricture/Barrett's metaplasia, aspiration pneumonia, apnea/near-miss event. Disk batteries/sharp FB/respiratory symptoms → urgent endoscopic removal.
MCQ pearls
  • Newborn, polyhydramnios, choking on first feed, NGT coils → esophageal atresia → screen VACTERL.
  • GERD best diagnostic test = 24h pH monitoring; metoclopramide is contraindicated in kids.
  • Barium "bird-beak" + dysphagia to fluids → achalasia.

Liver Diseases

Definition Hepatitis (viral, autoimmune, metabolic), cirrhosis, portal hypertension and inherited disorders (Wilson). Prolonged PT/INR is the earliest sign of active liver disease.

  • Portal HTN: >10 mmHg; cirrhosis = MC cause; → varices, splenomegaly/hypersplenism, ascites, encephalopathy.

Test Yourself — Gastrointestinal

Q1. A 6-month-old boy, 7 kg, has had 8 watery stools/day for 2 days. He is lethargic, has sunken eyes, capillary refill of 4 seconds and is hypotensive. What is the most appropriate first step?

  1. Oral rehydration solution 50 mL/kg over 4 hours
  2. IV bolus 20 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl over 20 minutes
  3. Start oral zinc and probiotics
  4. Maintenance fluids only at 100-50-20
Answer: B. This is severe dehydration with shock — give a 20 mL/kg bolus over 20 min. ORS (A) is contraindicated in shock; maintenance alone (D) ignores the deficit.

Q2. A 4-year-old develops bloody diarrhea after eating undercooked beef. Three days later he is pale, oliguric, with platelets 40,000 and rising creatinine. Which is the most appropriate management point?

  1. Start ciprofloxacin immediately
  2. Give an antidiarrheal to slow stool frequency
  3. Avoid antibiotics as they may worsen the condition
  4. Begin metronidazole for presumed amoebiasis
Answer: C. This is HUS from EHEC O157:H7 — antibiotics increase Shiga-toxin release and worsen HUS. Antidiarrheals (B) are not recommended in children.

Q3. A 3-week-old firstborn boy has progressive non-bilious projectile vomiting and remains hungry after vomiting. A 2 cm olive-shaped mass is felt in the epigastrium. ABG shows pH 7.52, low Cl and low K. What is the next best step?

  1. Immediate pyloromyotomy
  2. Upper GI contrast study to exclude volvulus
  3. Correct fluid and electrolyte imbalance, then pyloromyotomy
  4. Start a proton pump inhibitor for reflux
Answer: C. Pyloric stenosis with hypokalemic hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis — correct electrolytes/fluids first, then operate. Surgery before correction (A) is unsafe; vomiting is non-bilious, against volvulus (B).

Q4. A 9-month-old has episodes of colicky pain with drawing-up of the legs, becoming lethargic between attacks, and passes stool mixed with blood and mucus. A sausage-shaped mass is palpable. Which investigation is the best initial test?

  1. Abdominal ultrasound showing a target/doughnut sign
  2. Suction rectal biopsy
  3. 24-hour pH monitoring
  4. Anti-tissue transglutaminase antibody
Answer: A. Intussusception — US is the best initial test (target/doughnut sign); air enema is confirmatory and therapeutic. Rectal biopsy (B) is for Hirschsprung; tTG (D) is for celiac.

Q5. A 15-month-old presents 3 months after introduction of wheat with bulky pale offensive stools, abdominal distension, irritability and falling weight. Which combination is most appropriate for diagnosis?

  1. Stool culture and empirical antibiotics
  2. Anti-tTG IgA with serum IgA, then duodenal biopsy
  3. Sweat chloride test
  4. Hydrogen breath test alone
Answer: B. Classic celiac disease — anti-tTG IgA is the initial test (measure serum IgA to exclude deficiency), with duodenal biopsy as the gold standard. Hydrogen breath test (D) tests lactose intolerance.

3. Neurology

This system moves from paroxysmal events (febrile seizures, meningitis, epilepsy) to disorders of weakness and tone (acute flaccid paralysis, UMN lesions, the floppy infant), then to vascular disease (stroke) and a high-yield miscellany (neural tube defects, hydrocephalus, neurocutaneous syndromes, headache). The recurring exam skill is pattern discrimination: simple vs complex, UMN vs LMN, central vs peripheral, bacterial vs viral vs TB.

1. Febrile Seizure

Definition A seizure associated with fever in a child aged 6 months–5 years, with the focus of fever outside the CNS (no meningitis/encephalitis).
Why Genetic predisposition + fever lower the seizure threshold in the immature brain; >85% are viral (most commonly roseola/HHV-6), also URTI, OM, UTI, AGE, or recent immunization (MMR within 7–10 days, DTaP within 48h).
Presentation
  • Incidence 2–4% of children; slight male predominance; genetic predisposition 10–20%.
  • Post-ictal in febrile seizure: regains consciousness quickly, looks well, active (vs CNS infection: looks ill).
  • Recurrence 30–40%; highest risk in the 1st year after first attack. Major RFs: age <1y, fever <24h before seizure, lower temperature (38–39°C) at seizure.

2. Meningitis (links to Infectious system)

Definition Inflammation of the meninges; transmission usually by droplet. Bacterial mortality 5–10%; 10% of survivors have long-term neurological impairment.
Why Pathogens reach the CSF (a low-immunity space) and trigger neutrophilic inflammation that consumes glucose and raises protein — the basis of the CSF profile below.
Presentation
  • Infants: nonspecific (fever, poor feeding, irritability, vomiting) + bulging tense fontanelle, opisthotonus, petechial rash (meningococcal). Meningeal signs not reliable before 18 months.
  • Older children: classical triad — fever + altered mental status + meningismus (headache, photophobia, neck stiffness). Kernig and Brudzinski signs.
  • Late/raised ICP: Cushing triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular breathing).
Organism by age
  • Neonate (<1 month): Group B strep (MC), E. coli, Listeria.
  • 1 month–6 years: S. pneumoniae (MC), N. meningitidis, H. influenzae.
  • >6 years: S. pneumoniae (MC nowadays).
  • Viral (aseptic): enteroviruses (coxsackie) — MC cause of all meningitis in all groups.

3. Epilepsy

Definition ≥2 unprovoked seizures >24h apart without a clear cause. Status epilepticus: impending >5 min, established >30 min. Prevalence ~0.5% (1 in 200 children).
Why Most are idiopathic/primary (70–80%); secondary (20%) from infection (post-meningitis), trauma/hemorrhage, structural malformation, tumor, genetic/metabolic causes.
Presentation
  • Focal/partial: simple (consciousness intact, may have aura) vs complex (impaired); may secondarily generalize.
  • Generalized: tonic-clonic (commonest, no aura), absence, myoclonic, atonic.
  • Absence (petit mal): girls, age 4–10y, brief 5–40s staring without loss of postural tone, no aura/post-ictal, EEG 3-Hz spike-and-wave, provoked by hyperventilation.
  • Infantile spasm (West): salaam spasms, peak 3–8 months, triad of spasms + developmental delay + hypsarrhythmia; consider tuberous sclerosis.
  • Rolandic (benign centrotemporal): nocturnal focal seizures with facial/tongue symptoms, age 5–10y, resolves by 16; often no treatment.

4. Acute Flaccid Paralysis (GBS, polio, transverse myelitis)

Definition Sudden onset of weakness/paralysis — an emergency. Causes by site: anterior horn cell (polio, transverse myelitis), peripheral nerve (GBS, diphtheria), NMJ (botulism, tick), muscle (myositis).
GBS
  • MC cause of acute flaccid paralysis in healthy children; post-infectious autoimmune demyelinating polyneuropathy, 2/3 follow GI/RS infection (Campylobacter MC).
  • Ascending, symmetrical flaccid weakness + areflexia; bulbar involvement in 50%; dysautonomia in 70%.
  • LP: cyto-albumin dissociation (high protein >50, normal cells) after week 1. Nerve conduction = most specific.
  • Treatment: IVIG 2 g/kg (DOC) or plasmapheresis; steroids contraindicated; ICU if rapid progression/falling vital capacity. ~20% need ventilation; recovery 85–90%.
Transverse myelitis & Polio
  • Transverse myelitis: spinal cord inflammation, mean age 9y, asymmetrical leg weakness with a sensory level and early bladder involvement; UMN signs (brisk reflexes, +Babinski). CSF + MRI mandatory; IV methylprednisolone; prognosis worse than GBS. May herald MS.
  • Polio: oral-fecal spread, destroys anterior horn cells → asymmetric flaccid weakness, legs > arms, normal sensation, decreased reflexes, atrophy. Stool virus recovery is essential; supportive only.

7. Stroke

Definition Acute focal neurological deficit from a vascular mechanism with infarction/hemorrhage on imaging. Rare in children but among the top 10 causes of childhood death.
Why Multiple risk factors common (90% have ≥1). Sickle cell disease is the MC cause of pediatric stroke; also congenital cyanotic heart disease, prothrombotic deficiencies (protein S), vasculitis (SLE), moyamoya, arterial dissection.
Presentation
  • Ischemic (50%): MC type; MC site is the MCA (contralateral hemiplegia, dysphasia, hemianopia). Hemiparesis 60–80%, seizure 25–31%, headache 24–40%.
  • Hemorrhagic (50%): presents with headache + altered consciousness; AVM is the MC associated risk factor; more vomiting than ischemic.
  • Left-brain damage → right-sided paralysis (and vice versa).
Diagnosis CT first to rule out hemorrhage; MRI (diffusion-weighted) most sensitive for ischemia; MR angiogram for vessels. CBC, coagulation/thrombophilia profile, echo/ECG, vasculitis & metabolic screen.
Management
  • Neuroprotective: ABC, normoglycemia, correct dehydration, avoid hyperthermia, treat seizures aggressively, treat infection.
  • Arterial ischemic stroke: anticoagulation (heparin/LMWH) in select/cardiac causes; aspirin 3–5 mg/kg for secondary prevention; SCD → exchange transfusion.
  • CSVT: anticoagulation ~3 months. Thrombolysis NOT recommended by pediatric guidelines.
MCQ pearls
  • Child with sickle cell disease + acute hemiplegia → stroke → exchange transfusion.
  • MCA territory = contralateral arm-predominant hemiplegia + dysphasia.
  • Headache + ↓consciousness + AVM → hemorrhagic stroke.

8. Miscellaneous (Neural tube defects, hydrocephalus, neurocutaneous, headache)

Neural tube defects Failure of neural tube fusion in the first 28 days. Risk: folic acid deficiency, maternal DM, anticonvulsants, obesity. Prevention: folic acid 0.4 mg (all) or 4 mg (previously affected child), started 3 months pre-conception.
NTD spectrum
  • Spina bifida occulta: vertebral arch defect, intact cord; asymptomatic, overlying hair tuft/lipoma; cord tethering may occur later.
  • Meningocele: meninges only protrude, cord normal; good prognosis after repair.
  • Meningomyelocele: meninges + cord protrude (MC lumbosacral) → LMN leg paralysis, neuropathic bladder/bowel, and hydrocephalus from Arnold-Chiari II (80% need VP shunt). Close immediately after birth, multidisciplinary care.
Hydrocephalus Excess CSF enlarging ventricles. Obstructive (non-communicating): aqueduct stenosis, Dandy-Walker, Arnold-Chiari II. Communicating: overproduction (choroid plexus papilloma) or defective reabsorption (post-meningitis, post-hemorrhage). Infant: large head, bulging fontanelle, split sutures, sunset eyes. Treatment: VP shunt (complications: staph epidermidis infection, blockage, shunt nephritis).
Neurocutaneous syndromes
  • Neurofibromatosis 1 (AD, ch17): ≥6 café-au-lait spots, neurofibromas, axillary freckling, optic glioma, Lisch nodules, sphenoid dysplasia, 1st-degree relative.
  • Tuberous sclerosis (AD): ash-leaf depigmented patches, shagreen patch, adenoma sebaceum, cardiac rhabdomyomas, renal angiomyolipoma; cause of infantile spasms.
  • Sturge-Weber: port-wine stain (ophthalmic trigeminal), seizures, glaucoma, contralateral hemiplegia; MRI tram-line calcification.
Headache & Bell palsy
  • Migraine: commonest significant headache (10%), +ve family history; ≥5 attacks 4–72h, unilateral/pulsating/moderate-severe + nausea/photophobia; relieved by sleep. Acute: paracetamol/NSAID, triptans; prophylaxis: topiramate/valproate, propranolol (avoid in asthma).
  • Idiopathic intracranial hypertension: raised ICP, papilledema, normal MRI & LP; linked to vitamin A derivatives.
  • Bell palsy: isolated LMN 7th nerve palsy — whole half of face including forehead; prednisone within first week, eye care, acyclovir if herpes.
  • Red flags: headache worse lying down/coughing, wakes the child, morning vomiting, personality change, papilledema, abnormal gait → suspect raised ICP / tumor.
MCQ pearls
  • ≥6 café-au-lait spots + Lisch nodules → NF1.
  • Port-wine stain (V1) + seizures + tram-line calcification → Sturge-Weber.
  • Forehead involved in facial palsy = LMN (Bell); forehead spared = UMN.

Q1. An 8-month-old presents after a generalized tonic-clonic seizure lasting 5 minutes during a fever from roseola. He is now alert, smiling and feeding. Examination is normal. What is the most appropriate next step?

  2. Start prophylactic valproate
  3. Lumbar puncture to exclude meningitis
  4. Urgent CT brain and EEG
Answer: C. Although it looks like a simple febrile seizure, age <12 months mandates LP to exclude meningitis. CT/EEG are not indicated in simple febrile seizures, and prophylactic AEDs don't reduce recurrence.

Q2. A 4-year-old with fever, headache and neck stiffness has CSF showing turbid fluid, WBC >1000 (neutrophil-predominant), protein 180 mg/dL and glucose 25 mg/dL. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Viral (enteroviral) meningitis
  2. Bacterial meningitis
  3. Tuberculous meningitis
  4. Guillain-Barré syndrome
Answer: B. Neutrophilic pleocytosis with high protein and low glucose in turbid CSF is classic bacterial meningitis. Viral shows lymphocytes with normal glucose; TB shows lymphocytes with very high protein and a more chronic course.

Q3. A previously well 6-year-old develops symmetrical ascending leg weakness with absent reflexes two weeks after gastroenteritis. CSF shows raised protein with normal cell count. Which is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. IV immunoglobulin
  2. High-dose IV corticosteroids
  3. IV acyclovir
  4. Exchange transfusion
Answer: A. This is Guillain-Barré syndrome (cyto-albumin dissociation post-Campylobacter); DOC is IVIG 2 g/kg (or plasmapheresis). Steroids are contraindicated in GBS.

Q4. A 4-month-old boy is floppy with poor head control. He is alert with a normal facial expression, has tongue fasciculations and absent deep tendon reflexes. Creatine kinase is normal. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Spastic cerebral palsy
  2. Down syndrome
  3. Duchenne muscular dystrophy
  4. Spinal muscular atrophy (Werdnig-Hoffman)
Answer: D. Peripheral (LMN) hypotonia with tongue fasciculations, areflexia, preserved alertness and normal CK is classic SMA type I. CP gives UMN signs (hyperreflexia); Duchenne presents later with very high CK.

Q5. A 7-year-old with sickle cell disease presents with sudden right-sided weakness and dysphasia. CT shows no hemorrhage; MRI confirms a left MCA-territory infarct. In addition to supportive measures, which is the key specific intervention?

  1. IV thrombolysis (tPA)
  2. Exchange transfusion
  3. High-dose corticosteroids
  4. Immediate decompressive craniectomy
Answer: B. SCD is the MC cause of pediatric stroke; ischemic stroke due to SCD is treated with exchange transfusion to reduce sickle hemoglobin. Thrombolysis is not recommended by pediatric stroke guidelines.

4. Neonatology

The first 28 days of life and what threatens them: the problems of being born too early or too late (prematurity, postmaturity, IUGR), then the four big neonatal emergencies that recur on every paper — sepsis/TORCH, respiratory distress, jaundice, and the infant-of-diabetic-mother / birth-injury / HIE cluster. Master the comparison tables: respiratory distress (RDS vs TTN vs MAS), physiological vs pathological jaundice, and unconjugated vs conjugated causes are the highest-yield discriminators here.

Introduction, Prematurity & Postmaturity

Definition
  • Neonatal period = first 28 days (early = birth–7 d, late = 7–28 d).
  • Preterm <37 wk; term 37–42 wk; post-term >42 wk.
  • Weight: normal 2.5–4.5 kg; LBW <2500 g; VLBW <1500 g; ELBW <1000 g. SGA <10th centile, LGA >90th centile for GA.
Presentation
  • Preterm: thin shiny skin + lanugo, nails don't reach fingertips, soft ear with no recoil, smooth scrotum / undescended testes (boys) or prominent clitoris (girls), sole creases absent or cover only anterior 1/3, hypotonic frog posture, weak suck with incoordinate suck–swallow.
  • Post-term: alert open eyes, abundant scalp hair, dry wrinkled peeling skin with meconium staining, long nails, weight average or low.
  • Normal newborn skin (no Rx): milia, erythema toxicum (eosinophils, day 2–3), Mongolian spots (lumbosacral, DDx child abuse), witch's milk from maternal hormone withdrawal — never squeeze.
Diagnosis
  • Prematurity causes: idiopathic (most common, ~40%), then maternal/fetal/obstetric.
  • Newborn screening: heel-prick at day 5 — congenital hypothyroidism, sickle/thalassemia, CF, PKU, MSUD.
  • APGAR: 1 min = how to resuscitate, 5 min = response; score <7 → ABC.
  • IUGR — symmetrical (all parameters <10th, early/1st trimester, anomalies common → chromosomal/TORCH, bad prognosis) vs asymmetrical (head-sparing, late, placental insufficiency/maternal disease, good prognosis).
Management (preterm)
  • Radiant warmer → dry → neutral thermal environment (core 37 °C), NICU/incubator, IVF, vitamin K IM, antibiotics.
  • Antenatal steroids (dexa/betamethasone, 2 doses 12 h apart, 24–34+6 wk) cut RDS incidence/mortality ~40%.
  • O2 target: avoid <91% (↑NEC/death) and >95% (↑retinopathy of prematurity).
  • Trophic feeding 0.5–1 mL/kg/h; breastfeeding ↓NEC.
Red flags
  • NEC (4 P's: Prematurity, Perinatal asphyxia, Polycythemia, PDA): bilious vomiting + bloody stool + distension; X-ray pneumatosis intestinalis (specific) → NPO, NGT, IV cefotaxime + metronidazole.
  • IVH in first 72 h with severe RDS; pneumothorax → sudden deterioration, absent air entry → needle decompression.
MCQ pearls
  • Pneumatosis intestinalis = NEC; O2-dependence at 28 days = bronchopulmonary dysplasia.
  • Retinopathy of prematurity = prolonged high O2; mandatory ophthalmic screening (no warning sign).
  • Symmetrical IUGR → think TORCH / trisomy; asymmetrical → placental insufficiency.

IDM, Birth Injury & HIE

Definition
  • IDM = infant of a diabetic mother — macrosomic, plump, plethoric. HIE (perinatal asphyxia) = impaired gas exchange → hypoxemia, hypercarbia, acidosis (incidence ~0.5% of term newborns).
Why (IDM)
  • Maternal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperinsulinemia; at birth the glucose supply stops but insulin stays high → hypoglycemia (<45 mg/dL, ~50% of IDM, in first 24 h).
  • Insulin is anabolic → macrosomia (birth injury risk); chronic hypoxia → polycythemia → jaundice.
Presentation (IDM complications)
  • Hypoglycemia (jitteriness, apnea, seizures) — monitor sugar at ½–1–2–4–6–12–24 h; hypocalcemia (day 2, transient hypoparathyroidism); polycythemia (Hct >65%); indirect hyperbilirubinemia.
  • Anomalies: cardiac (TGA, VSD most common, ASD), NTD, renal agenesis, small left colon, sacral agenesis (most specific); transient hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (septal, resolves 6–12 m).
  • Birth injury (macrosomia >4.5 kg): shoulder dystocia, clavicular fracture (commonest), brachial plexus injury.

  2. Respiratory distress syndrome
  3. Meconium aspiration syndrome
  4. Congenital diaphragmatic hernia
Answer: B. Preterm + ground-glass/air bronchograms = surfactant-deficiency RDS. TTN occurs in term C/S babies with perihilar markings and self-resolves in 24–72 h.

Q2. A post-term infant is born through thick meconium-stained liquor and develops severe respiratory distress with cyanosis. CXR shows a hyperinflated chest with patchy consolidation. Which management step is contraindicated?

  1. Admission to NICU
  2. Surfactant via endotracheal tube
  3. CPAP
  4. Treatment of pulmonary hypertension
Answer: C. This is MAS — air trapping makes CPAP contraindicated (risk of pneumothorax); use O2/MV. Surfactant and PPHN treatment are appropriate.

Q3. A term breastfed infant presents at 6 weeks with persistent jaundice, dark urine and pale stools. Bilirubin is mainly conjugated. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Physiological jaundice
  2. Breast-milk jaundice
  3. Biliary atresia
  4. G6PD deficiency
Answer: C. Conjugated jaundice + pale stool + dark urine = biliary atresia → Kasai before 2 months. Physiological, breast-milk and G6PD jaundice are all unconjugated.

Q4. A macrosomic infant of a diabetic mother becomes jittery and has a seizure 3 hours after birth. Capillary glucose is 28 mg/dL. What is the most appropriate immediate management?

  1. IV bolus 5 mL/kg of D10% then D10% infusion
  2. Oral dextrose gel only
  3. IV calcium gluconate
  4. Partial exchange transfusion
Answer: A. Symptomatic neonatal hypoglycemia (<45 mg/dL) needs IV D10% bolus then infusion. Oral feeds are only for asymptomatic cases; calcium treats hypocalcemia (typically day 2).

Q5. A neonate born to a mother with first-trimester rash is found to have cataracts, sensorineural deafness and a continuous murmur (PDA). Skull CT shows no calcification. Which is the most likely congenital infection?

  1. Cytomegalovirus
  2. Toxoplasmosis
  3. Rubella
  4. Herpes simplex
Answer: C. Cataract + deafness + PDA with no calcification = congenital rubella. CMV gives periventricular calcification; toxoplasmosis gives diffuse calcification with hydrocephaly.

Measles

Definition Highly contagious airborne RNA paramyxovirus (single antigenic type); a major cause of childhood death in developing countries.
Presentation
  • Incubation 10–14 d; infectious 5 days before to 4 days after rash.
  • Prodrome (4 C + K): cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, high fever 40°C, Koplik spots.
  • Exanthem: maculopapular, starts behind ears/face → whole body in 24 h; then desquamation, resolving in order of appearance.
Diagnosis Clinical + serology (IgM).
Management Supportive (antipyretic, IV fluid) + Vitamin A (drug of choice). Prevent with MMR; airborne precautions, negative-pressure isolation.
Red flags Pneumonia, encephalitis, and the late fatal complication SSPE (subacute sclerosing panencephalitis).
MCQ pearls
  • Koplik spots precede the rash and are pathognomonic.
  • Give Vitamin A — reduces measles mortality.

Rubella & congenital rubella syndrome

Definition RNA virus (single antigenic type); droplet or transplacental spread (humans only host). Incubation 14–21 d.
Presentation
  • Prodrome: coryza, low-grade fever, postauricular & occipital lymphadenopathy, arthritis.
  • Eruptive: maculopapular rash like measles but no peeling, lasts 3 days; rose spots on soft palate + Forchheimer spots.
Red flags — Congenital rubella syndrome
  • Classic triad: cataract, sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), PDA.
  • Others: asymmetrical IUGR, hepatosplenomegaly, anemia/thrombocytopenia, direct (hepatitis) jaundice, microcephaly, chorioretinitis, blueberry-muffin rash.
Management Supportive only. Prevent with MMR (links to Neonatology TORCH).
MCQ pearls
  • Cataract + deafness + PDA in a small-for-dates neonate = congenital rubella.

Mumps

Definition RNA virus affecting the salivary (parotid) glands; airborne droplet spread, incubation 10–14 d.
Presentation Prodrome of fever/headache/malaise → parotitis: initially unilateral then bilateral, pain worse on chewing, pushes ears up and out; peaks day 2, subsides by day 5.
Diagnosis Clinical, serology, high serum amylase.
Red flags Meningoencephalitis (common), pancreatitis, transient SNHL, orchitis (post-puberty), oophoritis (rare).
Management Supportive; isolate 5 days after onset of swelling. Prevent with MMR.
MCQ pearls
  • Painful parotid swelling + raised amylase; post-pubertal boy → watch for orchitis.

Varicella (chickenpox) & shingles

Definition VZV (DNA); droplet/airborne + contact with skin lesions. Incubation 10–14 d; infectious 2 days before to 5 days after rash.
Presentation
  • Prodrome: fever, headache, anorexia, malaise.
  • Vesicular pruritic rash in crops over 3–5 d, head/trunk → periphery; all stages present at once (papule, vesicle, pustule, crust); heals without scar unless secondarily infected.
Management Antipyretic (not aspirin) + antipruritic; acyclovir if immunocompromised. Isolate until all lesions crust; varicella vaccine; VZIG for at-risk contacts.
Red flags 2° bacterial infection (necrotizing fasciitis, TSS), encephalitis/cerebellitis (ataxia), pneumonia, purpura fulminans, Reye's syndrome after aspirin.
Shingles (herpes zoster) Reactivation of latent VZV → dermatomal vesicular eruption (commonly thoracic); uncommon in children and rarely painful. Recurrent/multidermatomal shingles → suspect immunocompromise (HIV).
MCQ pearls
  • Never give aspirin in varicella/influenza → Reye's syndrome.
  • All lesion stages simultaneously distinguishes varicella from smallpox/other rashes.

Infectious mononucleosis (glandular fever)

Definition EBV (DNA); spread by saliva ("kissing disease"). Long incubation 30–60 days.
Presentation Fever, malaise, pharyngitis (mimics strep), cervical/generalized lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly; maculopapular rash typically after aminopenicillins (ampicillin).
Diagnosis CBC: high WBC with absolute lymphocytosis, atypical lymphocytes >20%; heterophile Ab (Monospot / Paul-Bunnell); EBV IgM (most specific); PCR.
Management Supportive; avoid ampicillin. (Avoid contact sport while splenomegaly persists.)
Red flags Airway obstruction, splenic rupture, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia/thrombocytopenia; linked to nasopharyngeal carcinoma & Burkitt lymphoma.
MCQ pearls
  • Sore throat + rash after amoxicillin/ampicillin = think EBV, not penicillin allergy.

Whooping cough (pertussis)

Definition Bordetella pertussis (gram-negative coccobacillus); droplet spread, highly contagious. Mainly infants <1 yr (peak 4 months); incubation 7–10 d.
Presentation — 3 stages
  • Catarrhal (1–2 wk): low-grade fever + coryza; most infectious.
  • Paroxysmal (2–6 wk): bursts of cough in one expiration → inspiratory whoop, cyanosis, post-tussive vomiting, gasping/apnea.
  • Convalescent (wk–months): "100-day cough".
Diagnosis CBC: high WBC with absolute lymphocytosis; nasopharyngeal swab; CXR.
Management Admit if severe/apneic or very young: O2, NGT/IV fluids, macrolides (DOC). Prevent: isolation, macrolide prophylaxis to contacts, DPT vaccine.
Red flags Apnea in young infants, bronchopneumonia, seizures, intracranial/subconjunctival haemorrhage, hernia/rectal prolapse.
MCQ pearls
  • Infant + whoop/post-tussive vomiting + lymphocytosis = pertussis; macrolide is the answer.

Scarlet fever

Definition Toxin-mediated GAS (S. pyogenes) infection; droplet spread, incubation 2–7 d.
Presentation Fever + pharyngitis; tongue white day 1 → red strawberry day 3; maculopapular sandpaper rash (neck → trunk/limbs) at 12–48 h with Pastia lines in flexures; red face with perioral pallor; fades with desquamation.
Diagnosis Throat swab C&S, CBC, high ASO titre/anti-DNase B.
Management Antipyretic + penicillin V for 10 days.
Red flags Post-streptococcal: PSGN, rheumatic fever, reactive arthritis.
MCQ pearls
  • Sandpaper rash + Pastia lines + strawberry tongue → 10-day penicillin prevents rheumatic fever.

Tuberculosis

Definition M. tuberculosis: aerobic, intracellular acid-fast bacillus; Ziehl-Neelsen stain, Löwenstein-Jensen culture. Droplet spread, insidious onset (~6 wk incubation).
Why Paediatric TB is less infectious, harder to diagnose, and progresses rapidly from latent to active disease vs adults.
Diagnosis — Mantoux (tuberculin) test
  • Type IV delayed hypersensitivity; intradermal 0.1 mL of 5-unit PPD; read induration (not erythema) at 48–72 h.
  • ≥5 mm +ve: close TB contact, clinical/radiological evidence, or immunosuppressed/HIV.
  • ≥10 mm +ve: high risk of disseminated disease (DM, CKD) or increased exposure.
  • ≥15 mm +ve: children with no risk factors.
  • False +ve: BCG. False −ve: malnutrition, HIV, disseminated TB, reading/injection error.
Presentation Often asymptomatic (50% infants, 90% older children) → found on routine testing and given chemoprophylaxis; otherwise primary/systemic disease.
Red flags — perinatal TB If mother has active disease: treat infant with INH, no separation, treat mother with anti-TB. If suspected TB at delivery: separate baby until maternal CXR obtained.
MCQ pearls
  • Induration cut-off depends on risk: 5 / 10 / 15 mm — know which child fits which.
  • Prior BCG is the classic cause of a false-positive Mantoux.

  3. Measles
  4. Varicella

  2. Vitamin A
  3. Penicillin V for 10 days
  4. Acyclovir
Answer: C. Sandpaper rash + Pastia lines + strawberry tongue = scarlet fever (GAS); a 10-day course of penicillin treats it and prevents rheumatic fever. Vitamin A is for measles; acyclovir is for varicella.

  3. Ceftriaxone
  4. Acyclovir
Answer: B. Whoop, post-tussive vomiting, apnea and lymphocytosis in an infant = pertussis (Bordetella pertussis); macrolides are the DOC and are also given to contacts.

  2. ASO titre

9. Nephrology

Covers the paediatric kidney from structural anomalies and UTI, through the two great glomerular syndromes (nephrotic vs nephritic), to renal failure (AKI/CKD), tubular defects and renal tumours — the exam pivots on telling these apart by urine findings, complement and age.

Introduction, anomalies & UTI

Definition The nephron (glomerulus + capsule + tubule) is the functional unit. Kidney roles: fluid/BP balance, electrolytes, acid–base, waste excretion, vitamin D activation, erythropoietin (last two explain CKD anaemia + renal rickets).
Why Newborn GFR is low (~25 ml/min/1.73m², lower in preterm) and only reaches adult values (~120) by age 2 — explaining why neonatal renal handling and drug clearance are immature. Schwartz GFR = height(cm) × K / serum Cr.
Sample collection & urinalysis
  • Suprapubic aspiration — infants/neonates (bladder is abdominal); any growth = positive.
  • Catheterisation — most practical when urgent; positive ≥10⁴ CFU/mL.
  • Clean-catch / midstream — older children; positive ≥10⁵ CFU/mL. Quick-wee technique aids clean-catch in infants.
  • Dipstick: nitrite +ve = UTI (−ve does NOT exclude); leukocyte esterase, protein (albumin-selective).
  • Casts: RBC casts → glomerular disease; WBC casts → pyelonephritis; hyaline = normal/fever/nephrotic.
  • Sterile pyuria (pyuria + negative culture): stone, TB, Kawasaki, SLE, appendicitis.
Congenital anomalies
  • VUR (incidence ~1%): retrograde urine bladder→ureter. Graded I–V by MCUG (I = ureter only; V = severe dilation + tortuosity + clubbing). Complications: pyelonephritis, scar, HTN, CKD.
  • Posterior urethral valves (PUV): males only, 1:5000–8000; poor urine stream/dribbling, bilateral hydronephrosis on antenatal US; Rx endoscopic valve ablation.
  • MCDK: non-functioning, no urine; half involute by 2 yrs; bilateral → Potter sequence.
  • PCKD: infantile = AR (bilateral loin masses, HTN, hepatic fibrosis); adult = AD.
  • Renal agenesis: bilateral = Potter syndrome (pulmonary hypoplasia, lethal).
  • Hypospadias (1:200–300): ventral meatus — do NOT circumcise (need tissue for repair). Undescended testis (~5% term): orchidopexy within 1 yr.
UTI — management
  • E. coli ~80% (most common). Proteus (more in boys, alkalinises urine); Klebsiella (neonates); adenovirus = haemorrhagic cystitis.
  • Cystitis: oral 3–5 days (cefixime, augmentin, cephalexin, nitrofurantoin, TMP/SMX).
  • Pyelonephritis: admit + IV (ceftriaxone older children; ampicillin+gentamicin/cefotaxime neonates) 5–14 days.
  • VUR prophylaxis (grades I–III, or if scar): nitrofurantoin / TMP-SMX / cephalexin single daily dose; grades IV–V → surgical reimplantation.
  • Imaging: US (initial — if normal, often no further work-up), MCUG (VUR), DMSA (scar).

Nephrotic & Nephritic syndromes

Definition Nephrotic = heavy proteinuria + generalised oedema + hypoalbuminaemia (<2.5 g/dL) + hyperlipidaemia (chol >250). Nephritic = haematuria + HTN + oliguria + non-heavy proteinuria + oedema.
Why In minimal change disease, podocyte foot-process effacement → GFB dysfunction → loss of albumin → low oncotic pressure (oedema) → liver upregulates protein + lipid synthesis (hyperlipidaemia). In nephritic disease, glomerular inflammation lets RBCs through (haematuria, RBC casts) and drops GFR (oliguria, HTN).
Presentation
  • Nephrotic: pitting oedema worse in mornings — periorbital (puffy eyes), face, limbs, scrotal/vulval, ascites, anasarca; foamy urine.
  • Nephritic (PSGN): cola-coloured painless haematuria, periorbital oedema, HTN (60–80%), oliguria, 1–3 wks after throat / 3–6 wks after skin GAS infection.
  • IgA nephropathy: macroscopic haematuria 1–3 days after URTI (vs PSGN's weeks) — most common chronic GN worldwide, males 3:1.
Diagnosis
  • Nephrotic: 24-h urine protein (gold standard), serum albumin <2.5, cholesterol >250, complement. Minimal change = selective proteinuria (>85% albumin), normal C3, EM foot-process effacement.
  • PSGN: ASO titre +ve, anti-DNAse +ve; low C3 with normal C4; dysmorphic RBCs + RBC casts; biopsy = subepithelial humps (EM), starry-sky IF.
  • Low C3 differential: PSGN (normal C4), MPGN, SLE (low C3+C4), shunt nephritis, subacute IE.
Management
  • Nephrotic (MCD): prednisolone 12 weeks (>90% remit), salt/fluid restriction, diuretics/albumin if hypovolaemic. Steroid-resistant/dependent/frequent relapse → cyclophosphamide or cyclosporine.
  • PSGN: supportive — >95% recover; bed rest, salt/fluid restriction, penicillin to eradicate strep; treat HTN (furosemide, ACEi; severe → IV hydralazine/nitroprusside).

Renal failure & Miscellaneous

Definition AKI = sudden, reversible loss of function (KDIGO: SCr ↑0.3 mg/dL in 48h, or ×1.5 baseline in 7d, or urine <0.5 ml/kg/h ×6h). CKD = gradual, irreversible (GFR <60 for >3 months).
Presentation
  • AKI causes: pre-renal (MC ~60% — dehydration, hypovolaemia, HF), renal (~30% — ATN, HUS, RVT, GN), post-renal (~10% — PUV, blocked catheter). Phases: oliguric → polyuric (loses Na/K, dehydration) → recovery.
  • CKD: growth retardation (FTT), normocytic-normochromic anaemia, renal osteodystrophy, HTN, uraemic syndrome (pericarditis, encephalopathy, platelet bleeding), polyuria/polydipsia.

  • Bartter (ascending loop of Henle): early/infancy, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, normal Mg, HIGH urinary Ca, normal/low BP.

Test Yourself — Nephrology

Q1. A 4-year-old boy develops cola-coloured urine, periorbital puffiness and BP 130/85 two weeks after a sore throat. C3 is low, C4 normal. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Minimal change nephrotic syndrome
  2. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis
  3. IgA nephropathy
  4. Alport syndrome
Answer: B. Nephritic picture (haematuria + HTN) ~1–3 weeks after pharyngitis with low C3, normal C4 = PSGN. IgA would follow URTI within days, not weeks.

Q2. A 3-year-old has generalised pitting oedema, puffy eyes, frothy urine, albumin 2.0 g/dL and cholesterol 320 mg/dL. BP is normal and C3 is normal. What is the best initial treatment?

  1. Oral prednisolone
  2. IV ceftriaxone
  3. Cyclophosphamide
  4. Renal biopsy then cyclosporine
Answer: A. Classic minimal change nephrotic syndrome (age, normal BP/C3) — start steroids (>90% respond); biopsy/second-line agents only if resistant, relapsing or atypical features.

Q3. A male neonate has a poor, dribbling urinary stream and antenatal ultrasound showed bilateral hydronephrosis. Which diagnosis must be excluded urgently?

  1. Vesicoureteral reflux
  2. Multicystic dysplastic kidney
  3. Posterior urethral valves
  4. Wilms tumour
Answer: C. Poor stream in a boy = PUV until proven otherwise; bilateral hydronephrosis/hydroureter supports it. Confirm with MCUG/endoscopy; untreated leads to VUR → scarring → CKD.

Q4. A dehydrated infant with vomiting has rising creatinine. Urine sodium is <20, FE Na is 0.5%, urine osmolality 600 and BUN:Cr ratio 25:1. Which best classifies the kidney injury?

  1. Chronic kidney disease
  2. Intrinsic (renal) AKI from ATN
  3. Post-renal obstruction
  4. Pre-renal AKI
Answer: D. Avid Na retention (low urine Na, FE Na <1%, high osmolality, BUN:Cr >20:1) = pre-renal AKI from hypovolaemia — treat with a 20 ml/kg fluid bolus. ATN would show FE Na >2%.

Q5. A 2-year-old is found to have a smooth, firm, unilateral abdominal mass that does not cross the midline, with painless haematuria and hypertension. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Neuroblastoma
  2. Hydronephrosis
  3. Wilms tumour
  4. Polycystic kidney disease
Answer: C. A well-circumscribed mass that does not cross the midline with haematuria and HTN = Wilms (nephroblastoma). Neuroblastoma is irregular, crosses the midline, raises urinary VMA/HVA and lacks haematuria.

6. Hematology

Pediatric blood disease: anemias sorted by red-cell size (microcytic, normocytic, macrocytic) and by mechanism (low production vs hemolysis), then bleeding disorders (platelet vs coagulation) and the childhood blood cancers (ALL, AML, lymphoma). The unifying exam skill is reading the CBC indices and blood film.

Anemia — framework & lab basics

Definition
  • Inadequate hemoglobin for age: neonate <14, 1–12 months <10, 1–12 years <11 g/dl.
  • Normal indices: MCV 80–100 fl, RDW 12–14%, retic <1%, RBC 4–6 million/mm³, platelets 150–450k.
Why
  • Classify by size (MCV → micro/normo/macrocytic) and by mechanism (decreased production vs increased destruction/hemolysis vs blood loss) — this pair drives the whole differential.
  • Physiologic anemia of newborn: birth oxygenation suppresses erythropoietin → preterm Hb falls from 14–22 to 9–10 by 6–12 weeks.
Blood-film clues (high-yield)
  • Target cell → thalassemia. Sickle cell → SCD. Spherocyte → hereditary spherocytosis / AIHA. Schistocyte → MAHA. Tear drop → thalassemia.
  • Heinz bodies + bite cells → G6PD. Howell–Jolly → SCD (asplenia). Basophilic stippling → lead. Iron granules → sideroblastic.
  • Mentzer index (MCV/RBC): <13 → thalassemia (RBC count preserved); >13 → IDA.
MCQ pearls
  • Signs of hemolysis (any cause): retic >2%, high LDH, high indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, dark urine, jaundice, pigmented gallstones.
  • Extravascular (spleen): spherocytes, splenomegaly, no hemoglobinuria — HS, AIHA, SCD, thalassemia, PK deficiency. Intravascular: hemoglobinuria, schistocytes, low haptoglobin, no splenomegaly — G6PD, MAHA (TTP/HUS), PNH, incompatibility.

Iron deficiency anemia (IDA)

Definition
  • Most common anemia in pediatrics; commonest cause is nutritional. Peak age 6–24 months. Iron absorbed in duodenum, carried on transferrin, stored as ferritin.
Why
  • Iron is needed to synthesize Hb and myoglobin; deficiency → small, pale cells.
Presentation & causes
  • Often asymptomatic; infant: pallor, poor feeding, fatigue. Older child: headache, dizziness, palpitations, poor concentration, pica, koilonychia, angular stomatitis/glossitis, flow murmur.
  • Causes: prolonged exclusive breastfeeding >6 m, cow's milk feeding (only 10% absorbed), low-iron diet, malabsorption (celiac, CMPI), blood loss (GERD, hookworm, Meckel's, heavy menses).
  • Avoid in excess: cow's milk, tea, high-fiber phytates. Iron-rich: red meat, liver, oily fish.
Diagnosis
  • CBC: microcytic hypochromic, high RDW. Iron study: serum iron low, ferritin low (most important), TIBC high, transferrin sat low.
Management
  • Oral iron (ferrous sulphate/gluconate), 3–6 mg/kg/day for 3–6 months (or 3 months after Hb normalizes). S/E: GI upset, constipation, dark stool.
  • Parenteral if malabsorption/poor compliance. PRBC transfusion only if severe (unstable, HF).
  • Response: appetite/concentration in 24–48 h; reticulocytosis day 5–7; Hb +1 g in 3–4 weeks; stores replete by 1–3 months.
MCQ pearls
  • Toddler on lots of cow's milk + pallor + microcytic high-RDW + low ferritin = classic IDA. First retic rise confirms iron response at ~day 5–7.

Thalassemia (α and β)

Definition
  • AR defect in globin chain synthesis. α-chain gene on Ch.16 (4 genes), β-chain gene on Ch.11 (2 genes).
  • β-thalassemia major (Cooley's) = commonest chronic hemolytic anemia in the Mediterranean/Middle East and commonest transfusion-dependent anemia.
Spectrum
  • α: silent carrier (normal CBC) → trait (mild microcytic) → Hb H (microcytic + mild hemolysis) → Hydrops fetalis (- -/- -): severe anemia, anasarca, HF.
  • β: trait/minor (asymptomatic, mild microcytic, no Rx) → intermedia (moderate) → major (β/β): severe hemolysis, transfusion dependent, iron overload.
  • Major: severe anemia >6 months, chronic hemolysis (pallor, jaundice, splenomegaly, GB stones), thalassemic face (frontal bossing, maxillary prominence), FTT, aplastic crisis (parvovirus B19).
Diagnosis
  • CBC: microcytic hypochromic, RDW normal/high, retic >2%, Mentzer <13. Film: target cells, tear drops.
  • Hb electrophoresis (diagnostic) in β-major: ↓Hb A, ↑Hb F, ↑Hb A2. Skull X-ray: hair-on-end.
Management
  • Monthly transfusion to keep Hb >9.5 g/dl (allows growth, suppresses extramedullary hematopoiesis). Folic acid.
  • Iron chelation when ferritin >1000: deferoxamine (injection) or deferasirox (tablet).
  • Splenectomy (after age 5–6, vaccinate first, lifelong penicillin). BM transplant = curative.
Red flags
  • Iron overload from chronic hemolysis + transfusion: cirrhosis, bronze DM (pancreas), cardiomyopathy, skin pigmentation, pituitary infiltration (delayed puberty, FTT).
MCQ pearls
  • Mediterranean child, microcytic with normal/high RBC count, Mentzer <13, target cells, normal ferritin = thalassemia (not IDA). ↑Hb A2 confirms β-trait.

Sickle cell disease

Definition
  • AR, more common in black children. Structural defect in β-chain: valine replaces glutamic acid at position 6 → Hb S.
Why
  • Under hypoxia Hb S polymerizes → sickling → vascular occlusion (ischemia) + rigid cells → premature hemolysis.
Crises
  • Presents >6 months. Chronic hemolytic features + crises.
  • Vaso-occlusive (painful) crisis: most common & specific; triggers hypoxia/fever/acidosis/infection/dehydration. Dactylitis (hand-foot) very common <2 y. Acute chest syndrome (fatal): SOB, chest pain, fever, CXR patches. CNS stroke, priapism, AVN femoral head, auto-splenectomy.
  • Aplastic crisis: sudden pancytopenia, parvovirus B19. Sequestration: pooling in spleen → massive splenomegaly + hypovolemic shock.
Diagnosis
  • CBC: normocytic normochromic; retic >2%, high LDH, high indirect bili. Film: sickle cells, Howell–Jolly bodies. Hb electrophoresis (diagnostic): ↑Hb S 80–95%, ↑Hb F, absent Hb A.
Management
  • Daily folic acid; hydroxyurea (raises Hb F long-term). Vaccinate against encapsulated bacteria + prophylactic oral penicillin to age 5.
  • VOC: analgesia (morphine/ibuprofen), IV hydration, O2, empiric antibiotics; transfusion only if severe. Aplastic/sequestration/hemolytic crisis → transfusion. Stem cell transplant = curative.
MCQ pearls
  • Dactylitis in a toddler = classic first SCD presentation. Parvovirus B19 → aplastic crisis. Trait (Hb AS) is asymptomatic and protects against malaria.

G6PD deficiency & hereditary spherocytosis

G6PD deficiency
  • Most common RBC enzyme disorder, X-linked recessive (males affected, females carriers).
  • Triggers (oxidative stress): fava beans (favism), infection, drugs (sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, dapsone, primaquine, aspirin), vitamin K.
  • Presents with neonatal jaundice (first 24 h) or acute hemolysis 24–72 h after trigger: dark urine, jaundice, pallor; self-limited in 8–14 days.
  • Labs: normocytic normochromic + hemolysis markers; film Heinz bodies, bite cells; enzyme assay (do 2–3 months after attack to avoid false negative). Rx: stop oxidant, transfuse if severe; prevention by avoidance.
Hereditary spherocytosis
  • Autosomal dominant (25% new mutation). Defect in membrane proteins spectrin/ankyrin → spherical, rigid cells removed by spleen (extravascular hemolysis).
  • Neonatal indirect jaundice, chronic hemolysis, splenomegaly, gallstones; aplastic crisis with parvovirus B19.
  • Labs: normocytic normochromic, retic >2%, film spherocytes, Coombs negative (distinguishes from AIHA), osmotic fragility test diagnostic (also EMA flow cytometry).
  • Rx: folic acid + follow-up; transfuse if severe; splenectomy after age 5–6 (vaccinate first, then lifelong penicillin); cholecystectomy if symptomatic stones.
MCQ pearls
  • Boy + jaundice/dark urine after fava beans or sulfa drug + bite cells/Heinz bodies = G6PD.
  • Spherocytes + Coombs NEGATIVE = HS; spherocytes + Coombs POSITIVE = AIHA.

Macrocytic & aplastic anemias

Megaloblastic (B12 / folate)
  • B12: animal products only, stores 2–3 yrs, absorbed terminal ileum; deficiency in vegans, pernicious anemia, Crohn/ileal resection. Only B12 causes neuropathy (subacute combined degeneration, sensory ataxia, optic atrophy).
  • Folate: in vegetables, stores 3–4 months; low in goat's milk diet, celiac, high utilization (hemolysis, pregnancy), antifolate drugs (phenytoin, MTX, trimethoprim).
  • Labs: macrocytic, hypersegmented neutrophils; serum B12/folate diagnostic. Rx: oral folate 1–5 mg; B12 (hydroxocobalamin) IM.
Aplastic anemia (BM failure)
  • Pancytopenia + hypoplastic marrow: anemia, infections, bruising; no lymphadenopathy, no hepatosplenomegaly (helps distinguish from leukemia). Causes: idiopathic, chloramphenicol, irradiation, infection; inherited (Fanconi, Schwachman-Diamond).
  • Fanconi anemia: most common inherited aplastic anemia, AR, onset 5–6 y, pancytopenia + anomalies (short stature, microcephaly, café-au-lait, absent/abnormal thumb-radii, horseshoe kidney, CHD), macrocytic, chromosome breakage test, high leukemia risk.
  • Rx: supportive (PRBC/platelets, G-CSF), androgens, stem cell transplant.
MCQ pearls
  • Macrocytic + neuropathy = B12 (folate has no neuropathy). Pancytopenia with no organomegaly/nodes → aplastic; with organomegaly/nodes → leukemia.

8. Endocrinology

Hypothalamic-pituitary control of growth and the thyroid/parathyroid, adrenal and pancreatic axes — where congenital hypothyroidism, DKA and CAH are the high-yield emergencies and short stature ties the growth-axis story together.

Growth Hormone & Hypopituitarism (GHD)

Definition Underproduction of GH alone or with other anterior pituitary hormones; deficiency of all AP hormones = panhypopituitarism. Incidence ~1 in 4000 children.
Why GH (191-AA polypeptide from somatotrophs) drives linear growth via liver-derived IGF-1; it is secreted in pulses, so a random GH level is nearly useless.
Presentation
  • Neonate: normal length/weight at birth, severe persistent hypoglycaemia, apnoea/cyanosis, micropenis (<2 cm stretched), prolonged jaundice, midline defects (cleft palate, septo-optic dysplasia).
  • Child: short stature with delayed bone age (appears end of 2nd year), childish/immature face, truncal obesity, delayed puberty, hypoglycaemia — normal IQ.
Diagnosis
  • GH provocation/stimulation test (insulin, glucagon, clonidine, arginine, exercise) — random GH not useful.
  • Low IGF-1 and low IGF-binding protein; other pituitary hormones (TFT).
  • Delayed bone age on left hand/wrist X-ray; MRI pituitary if aplasia/hypoplasia suspected.
Management
  • Recombinant GH — start as early as possible in classic GHD; replace other hormones if panhypopituitarism.
  • Also licensed in: Turner (45XO), CKD, Prader-Willi, idiopathic SS, IUGR.
  • Recombinant IGF-1 for GH resistance (Laron syndrome) / IGF-1 deficiency.
  • S/E: pseudotumor cerebri, slipped upper femoral epiphysis, gynaecomastia, insulin resistance, leukaemia risk.
MCQ pearls
  • Neonatal hypoglycaemia + micropenis + midline defect → congenital hypopituitarism.
  • Short stature with normal IQ = GHD; low IQ points to hypothyroidism.
  • Laron = GH receptor defect (high GH, low IGF-1) → treat with IGF-1, not GH.

Acquired Hypothyroidism & Hyperthyroidism

Definition Acquired hypothyroidism is commoner than hyperthyroidism; ~99% is primary. Hashimoto thyroiditis is the most common cause and the MC cause of sporadic goiter in childhood/adolescence.
Presentation — Hashimoto
  • More in females, age >6 yrs, +ve family history; autoimmune (anti-TPO, anti-TG, TSH-R blocking Ab).
  • Asymptomatic painless goiter (MC) + hypothyroid features: slow growth, weight gain, dry skin, cold intolerance, constipation, bradycardia, hyporeflexia; may transiently start hyperthyroid.
  • Associations: DMT1, Addison, vitiligo, pernicious anaemia, coeliac; Down, Turner.
Presentation — Graves
  • Causes 95% of childhood hyperthyroidism; stimulatory TSH-R antibodies; teenage females.
  • Anxiety, high appetite + weight loss, sweating, diarrhoea, rapid growth + advanced bone age, tremor, tachycardia, warm extremities, goiter; eye signs (exophthalmos) uncommon in children.
Management
  • Hashimoto: levothyroxine. Graves: 1st line medical = carbimazole or PTU + propranolol for tremor/HR/anxiety; then surgery or radioiodine.
  • Transient neonatal hyperthyroidism (maternal TSH-R Ab) — potentially fatal, treat; resolves as antibodies clear.
MCQ pearls
  • Goiter + weight loss + tachycardia + advanced bone age in a teenage girl → Graves.
  • Hyperthyroid TFT = high T3/T4, low TSH; treat tremor/tachycardia with propranolol.

Adrenal Insufficiency, Crisis & Cushing

Presentation — Addison / crisis
  • Addison = primary autoimmune adrenal insufficiency; associated DMT1, coeliac, Hashimoto, vitiligo. Fatigue, weight loss, postural hypotension, skin hyperpigmentation.
  • Adrenal crisis: vomiting/diarrhoea, dehydration, vascular collapse/shock; low Na (<130), high K (>5), low glucose (<46), metabolic acidosis.
Management — crisis
  • ABC + 3S: Saline (20 ml/kg NS), Steroid (IV hydrocortisone), Sugar (D10% 2–5 ml/kg).
  • Treat hyperkalaemia: IV calcium gluconate (cardiac protection), salbutamol, insulin + dextrose.
Presentation — Cushing
  • Excess corticosteroid; exogenous steroids = MC cause.
  • Central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, growth failure (short stature + low velocity), striae, easy bruising, HTN, hyperglycaemia.
Diagnosis — Cushing
  • High 24-h urinary free cortisol; loss of diurnal variation; dexamethasone suppression.
  • High-dose dex: suppresses → Cushing disease (pituitary); no suppression → ectopic ACTH (high ACTH) or adrenal tumor (low ACTH).
MCQ pearls
  • Obesity WITH short stature/poor growth = pathological (Cushing); simple obesity → tall + advanced bone age.
  • Crisis triad to memorise: low Na, high K, low glucose, acidosis → 3S resuscitation.

7. Rheumatology

Two high-yield clusters: the limping/painful-joint child (separating septic arthritis from transient synovitis and chronic JIA), and pediatric vasculitis (HSP and Kawasaki dominate both exams and clinic).

Approach to a child with arthritis

Definition
  • Inflammatory arthritis = infectious (septic arthritis) or non-infectious (reactive arthritis, JIA).
  • Non-inflammatory joint pain (not true arthritis) = Legg-Calve-Perthes, SCFE, growing pains.
  • Chronic = symptoms lasting ≥6 weeks; insidious onset, pain/swelling/limited ROM without redness, worse in the morning (morning stiffness & gelling).
Why
  • Acute red hot joint that cannot bear weight = septic until proven otherwise (pus under pressure destroys cartilage in hours).
  • Morning stiffness improving through the day points to inflammatory/chronic disease (overnight cytokine accumulation), not mechanical pain.

Septic arthritis

Presentation
  • Acute, monoarticular; most common <5 years and adolescents.
  • Fever, redness, swelling, warmth, tenderness; decreased ROM, limping, inability to bear weight; pain on movement (e.g. with diaper change).
  • Joints: knees 40% > hip 20% > ankle 14%. Hip held in flexion + external rotation; knee/ankle held in flexion.
  • Spread is usually hematogenous.
Diagnosis
  • Synovial fluid aspiration → Gram stain & culture (PCR for Kingella if culture-negative).
  • CBC: elevated WBC, CRP, ESR. Blood culture positive in ~50%.
  • Imaging: US (effusion), X-ray (increased joint space), MRI with contrast (synovitis).
  • Adolescents: screen for N. gonorrhoeae (urethral/cervical/rectal/pharyngeal culture or NAAT).
Management
  • Empiric IV antibiotics by age/Gram stain/culture. S. aureus is the most common organism overall.
  • <2 mo: GBS (ampicillin + aminoglycoside), S. aureus (nafcillin/cefazolin/vancomycin), E. coli/Klebsiella (cefotaxime).
  • 2 mo–maturity: cover S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, GAS, Kingella; N. gonorrhoeae → ceftriaxone.
  • Duration ~2–3 weeks, guided by ESR/CRP; may switch to oral once responding.
Red flags
  • Septic arthritis of the hip is an EMERGENCY → urgent incision and drainage.
  • Organisms by age: <2 mo GBS/S. aureus/E. coli/Klebsiella; older child S. aureus/S. pneumoniae/GAS/Kingella; consider Lyme (Borrelia) and gonococcus.

Presentation
  • Most common cause of acute hip pain in children, age 2–12 yrs, often after URTI/GI infection.
  • Sudden hip pain (may refer to knee), limited ROM (esp. extreme abduction/internal rotation), no pain at rest, no fever, child not ill-appearing.
  • CBC and CRP normal; improves within days (self-limited).

Vasculitis (HSP & Kawasaki)

Definition
  • Vasculitis = inflammation of the vessel wall; multisystem. Primary (autoimmune) or secondary (infection, malignancy, drugs, SLE/JDM).
  • In children, HSP (49%) and Kawasaki (23%) are the most common; PAN is 3rd (3%).
Why
  • HSP = IgA immune-complex deposition in small vessels → palpable purpura, GI and renal involvement.
  • Kawasaki = medium-vessel vasculitis with tropism for the coronary arteries → the dreaded aneurysms; treat fast to protect the heart.
  • ESR/CRP are markedly elevated; diagnostic gold standard for most vasculitides is biopsy (but HSP and Kawasaki are clinical diagnoses).

Henoch-Schonlein purpura (HSP)

Presentation
  • Most common systemic vasculitis of childhood; peaks 3–8 yrs, winter, M>F 2:1; often follows URI/strep (70–80%). IgA-mediated small-vessel.
  • Rash: palpable, non-blanching purpura/petechiae, lower-limb & dependent/pressure areas, in crops.
  • Arthritis/arthralgia (>2/3): knees & ankles, non-destructive, resolves <1 wk.
  • GI (2/3): colicky abdominal pain; watch for intussusception (<5%, key complication).
  • Renal (1/3): hematuria/proteinuria; proteinuria most predictive of nephritis; risk highest in first 3 months.
  • Angioedema (buttocks/scalp), scrotal swelling (13%, mimics torsion).
Diagnosis
  • Clinical: purpura (lower-limb predominant) + ≥1 of: arthritis/arthralgia, abdominal pain, renal involvement, or biopsy showing IgA deposition.
  • No diagnostic test: CBC (WBC/platelets normal or high — normal/high platelets, NOT low), ESR variable, serum IgA up in ½; ANA, RF, ANCA all negative; coagulation normal.
  • Biopsy (if needed): skin = leukocytoclastic angiitis with IgA; kidney = mesangial IgA.
Management
  • Self-limited, excellent prognosis; mainly supportive — NSAIDs for joints, bowel rest for GI.
  • Steroids (1–2 mg/kg/day) for severe GI/CNS disease or refractory cases.
  • Serial UA screening for first 3–6 months; refer nephrology if persistent hematuria, proteinuria, or HTN.
  • Poor prognosis: nephrotic/nephritic picture in first 6 mo, or low factor XIII activity.

Kawasaki disease

Presentation
  • More in Asians (esp. Japan), peak Feb–May, young children (US incidence 6:100,000 in <5 yrs).
  • Acute phase: high fever + irritability, conjunctival erythema, mucous-membrane changes, cervical node, extremity swelling, rash.
  • Subacute (1–2 wks): fever resolves, periungual desquamation, thrombocytosis.
Diagnosis
  • Fever ≥5 days PLUS ≥4 of 5 principal criteria (no other explanation).
  • Incomplete KD: fever + only 2–3 criteria — supported by labs: albumin ≤3 g/dL, anemia, elevated ALT, platelets ≥450,000 after day 7, WBC ≥15,000, sterile pyuria (UA WBC ≥10/HPF).
  • Echocardiogram in acute phase, then at 2–3 wks and convalescent phase (look for coronary aneurysms).

Test Yourself — Rheumatology

Q1. A 3-year-old has refused to walk for 1 day. He is febrile (39°C), holds the right hip in flexion and external rotation, and screams on any movement. CRP and WBC are markedly elevated. Which is the most appropriate next step?

  1. Reassure and review in 1 week
  2. Start oral ibuprofen for transient synovitis
  3. Urgent joint aspiration with Gram stain and culture
  4. Plain X-ray and discharge if normal
Answer: C. Fever, inability to bear weight, and high inflammatory markers indicate septic arthritis — aspirate to confirm and guide antibiotics (hip septic arthritis is a surgical emergency). Transient synovitis (B) would have normal labs and no fever; an early X-ray (D) is often normal and cannot exclude infection.

Q2. A 5-year-old has high spiking daily fevers for 3 weeks, a salmon-pink rash that comes and goes with the fever, hepatosplenomegaly, and arthritis of both knees. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Systemic-onset JIA
  2. Oligoarticular JIA
  3. Reactive arthritis
  4. Henoch-Schonlein purpura
Answer: A. Daily (quotidian) fever, evanescent salmon-pink rash, organomegaly and arthritis are the defining picture of systemic-onset JIA. Oligoarticular JIA (B) is afebrile with ≤4 joints; HSP (D) presents with palpable purpura, not a salmon rash.

Q3. A 6-year-old develops palpable, non-blanching purpura over the buttocks and lower legs, colicky abdominal pain, and painful swollen ankles two weeks after a sore throat. Platelets and coagulation studies are normal. Which test best supports the diagnosis if biopsy is performed?

  1. Low platelet count
  2. Positive ANCA
  3. IgA deposition in small vessels
  4. Positive rheumatoid factor
Answer: C. This is HSP, an IgA-mediated small-vessel vasculitis; biopsy shows IgA deposition (skin leukocytoclastic angiitis, mesangial IgA in kidney). Platelets are normal/high not low (A), and ANCA/RF are negative in HSP (B, D).

Q4. A 2-year-old has had fever for 6 days plus bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, cracked red lips with a strawberry tongue, a truncal rash, and swollen hands. Which intervention most reduces his risk of long-term cardiac complications?

  1. Oral corticosteroids alone
  2. IVIG plus aspirin
  3. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics
  4. Supportive care and NSAIDs only
Answer: B. Fever ≥5 days plus 4 of 5 CRASH criteria = Kawasaki disease; IVIG (2 g/kg) plus aspirin drops the coronary aneurysm rate from ~15–20% to ~2–4%. Antibiotics (C) and supportive care alone (D) do not protect the coronaries.

Q5. A previously well 7-year-old has had a limp for 6 hours after a recent cold. He is afebrile, looks well, and has mildly limited hip internal rotation; CBC and CRP are normal. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Septic arthritis of the hip
  2. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis
  3. Legg-Calve-Perthes disease
  4. Transient synovitis
Answer: D. An afebrile, well-appearing child with a post-viral limp and normal inflammatory markers fits transient synovitis (self-limited, resolves in days). Septic arthritis (A) would be febrile with high CRP; SCFE (B) affects overweight adolescents; Perthes (C) causes a more chronic painless limp with X-ray changes.

11. Genetics

How genetic disease is classified (chromosomal vs single-gene vs multifactorial vs mitochondrial) and the inheritance patterns that drive recurrence risk, then the high-yield syndromes — trisomies and sex-chromosome disorders are the exam's bread and butter.

Introduction to genetic diseases

Definition Genetic disease arises from abnormalities at the chromosomal level (number or structure) or the single-gene level (Mendelian), plus multifactorial (gene + environment) and mitochondrial (maternally inherited) categories.
Why Somatic cells are diploid, 46 chromosomes (44 autosomes + 2 sex: XX or XY); germ cells are haploid, 23. Karyotyping (46 XX / 46 XY) studies chromosome number, size and shape; FISH localises a specific gene/DNA sequence; PCR = DNA analysis. The short arm = p, long arm = q, joined at the centromere.
Indications for cytogenetic analysis
  • Postnatal: newborn with birth defect, learning disability, dysmorphic features, 1 major or ≥3 minor congenital anomalies, infertility/recurrent abortion/primary amenorrhea, ambiguous genitalia.
  • Prenatal: abnormal U/S, increased Down risk, previous chromosomally abnormal child, parent carrying a structural rearrangement.
Types of genetic disease
  • Chromosomal — numerical: polyploidy (triploidy 69, tetraploidy 92); aneuploidy — trisomy (T21, Klinefelter XXY), monosomy (Turner XO); mosaicism (2 cell lines, one trisomic + one normal).
  • Chromosomal — structural: translocation (Robertsonian), deletion, duplication, inversion.
  • Single-gene (Mendelian): AD, AR, X-linked recessive (XLR), X-linked dominant (XLD).
  • Multifactorial (polygenic): gene + environment; risk highest in 1st-degree relatives, rises with number/severity of affected members, sex ratio may be unequal (pyloric stenosis > in males). Examples: cleft lip/palate, CHD, pyloric stenosis, NTD, hypospadias, DDH, talipes, epilepsy, DM, HTN, autism, atopy (asthma/eczema).
  • Mitochondrial: maternally inherited (only ovum has cytoplasm); affected mother's sons do not transmit. Examples: Pearson, Leigh disease, KSS.

Definition Recognisable patterns of malformation — most commonly the autosomal trisomies (21, 18, 13), the sex-chromosome aneuploidies (Turner XO, Klinefelter XXY), and microdeletion/single-gene syndromes.

Other high-yield syndromes
  • Noonan (AD, normal karyotype): Turner-like phenotype in both sexes, webbed neck, pulmonary stenosis / HOCM, pectus excavatum.

  • Prader-Willi (microdeletion 15, paternal): hypotonia, hypogonadism, hyperphagia/obesity, almond eyes.
  • Angelman (microdeletion 15, maternal): uncontrolled laughter, ataxia, seizures, open mouth.
  • DiGeorge (AD, microdeletion 22 — CATCH): CHD, hypertelorism, thymic aplasia, cleft palate, hypocalcaemia.
  • William's (microdeletion 7): elfin face, supravalvular AS, hypercalcaemia, overly friendly personality.
  • Beckwith-Wiedemann (microdeletion 11): macrosomia, macroglossia, omphalocele, Wilms tumour, hypoglycaemia.
  • Cri-du-chat (microdeletion 5): cat cry, hypotonia, micrognathia.

  • Down: sudden atlanto-axial dislocation → sudden death; bilious vomiting (duodenal atresia); leukaemia.

  • Turner-like but normal karyotype + pulmonary stenosis = Noonan.
  • AVSD is the MC heart defect in Down; coarctation/bicuspid AV in Turner.

Vitamin D & Rickets

Definition Rickets = a metabolic disorder of infancy/childhood due to failure of mineralization of osteoid in growing bone. Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin in two natural forms — D3 (cholecalciferol) from skin UV irradiation of 7-dehydrocholesterol, and D2 from UV irradiation of plant ergosterol.
Why — activation & action
  • D3/D2 are inactive → liver hydroxylation to 25-OH vit D (calcidiol) → kidney hydroxylation to 1,25-(OH)2 vit D (calcitriol), the active form.
  • Calcitriol: ↑ gut Ca & phosphate absorption, ↑ renal Ca & phosphate reabsorption, regulates bone Ca/phosphate turnover.
  • Daily requirement: 400 IU/day (term breastfed) and 800–1000 IU/day (preterm). Breast and cow's milk are poor in vitamin D.
Presentation
  • Head: craniotabes (1st sign) — ping-pong skull softening over the occiput; wide/delayed anterior fontanelle; frontal bossing (square head); macrocephaly; delayed dentition + enamel hypoplasia.
  • Chest: rachitic rosary (costochondral cartilage proliferation); pigeon chest; Harrison sulcus (groove at diaphragm insertion).
  • Extremities: widened wrist/ankle joints (metaphyseal expansion); genu varum (bow legs) if walking, varus/valgus forearm if crawling; Marfan sign at medial malleolus.
  • Neuro: hypocalcemic tetany (carpopedal spasm, laryngospasm, convulsions), hypotonia, delayed milestones.
  • Spine deformities (kyphosis/scoliosis/lordosis), short stature, pathological fractures.
Diagnosis
  • Labs: Ca ↓ or normal (compensatory hyperPTH), phosphate ↓ (or ↑ in renal rickets), ALP ↑ (most sensitive for activity), PTH ↑. Normal Ca 8.5–10.5 mg/dL; ALP 50–150 U/L.
  • Best vit D measure: serum 25-OH vit D (deficient < 20, insufficient 20–29, normal 30–100 ng/mL).
  • X-ray wrist (best for early dx): triad of widening, cupping, fraying, frayed/cupped "paintbrush" metaphysis, widened growth plate, loss of provisional calcification zone.
Management
  • Vitamin D3: single IM high dose 600,000 IU, OR oral 3000–5000 IU/day, then daily maintenance. Bone healing begins in 2–4 weeks (deformities may take years).
  • Ca gluconate if hypocalcemic (oral, or IV if symptomatic); treat complications.
  • Prevention: sunlight, vit-D–rich diet, daily 400 IU (preterm twins 1000 IU), avoid rachitogenic diet.
Red flags Excess vitamin D therapy → hypervitaminosis D: ↑ serum & urinary Ca, anorexia/vomiting/constipation, polyuria/polydipsia, renal stones, nephrocalcinosis. Treat by stopping vit D & Ca, IV fluids, steroids.

Test Yourself — Nutrition & Growth

Q1. A 9-month-old exclusively breastfed infant, kept indoors and swaddled through winter, has a soft compressible occiput, widened wrists, and beading along the costochondral junctions. Serum ALP is markedly raised. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Osteogenesis imperfecta
  2. Vitamin D deficiency rickets
  3. Scurvy
  4. Achondroplasia
Answer: B. Exclusive breastfeeding (low vit D) + the "3 W's" (wrapping, winter, windows) + craniotabes, rachitic rosary and raised ALP are classic nutritional rickets. Scurvy causes bleeding gums, not these bony mineralization signs.

Q2. A 2-year-old from a region of famine has generalized pitting edema, a "moon" face, a hypopigmented "flag-sign" in his hair, and hepatomegaly. His weight appears near-normal because of the swelling. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Marasmus
  2. Nephrotic syndrome
  3. Kwashiorkor
  4. Congestive heart failure
Answer: C. Edema + fatty liver + flag-sign hair with relatively preserved fat is protein-deficiency kwashiorkor (hypoalbuminemia → ↓ oncotic pressure). Marasmus is calorie deficiency with severe wasting and no edema.

Q3. A healthy term infant weighed 3.2 kg at birth. Assuming normal growth, what is the expected weight at 1 year of age?

  1. ~6.4 kg (double)
  2. ~9.6 kg (triple)
  3. ~12.8 kg (quadruple)
  4. ~4.8 kg
Answer: B. Birth weight triples by 1 year (doubles by 6 months, quadruples by 2 years). 3.2 × 3 ≈ 9.6 kg. Doubling (A) is the 6-month value; quadrupling (C) is the 2-year value.

Q4. A 7-month-old infant, well on breast milk, develops persistent watery diarrhea each time the mother trials a cow's-milk formula, but is symptom-free back on the breast. Which is the most likely cause?

  1. Cow's milk protein allergy
  2. Celiac disease
  3. Galactosemia
  4. Constitutional growth delay
Answer: A. Thriving on breast milk but diarrhea triggered specifically by cow's-milk formula is the sheet's signature of cow's-milk protein allergy. Celiac would appear after introducing wheat/cereal, not formula.

Q5. A toddler treated for rickets has been kept on high-dose oral vitamin D for many months. He now has anorexia, vomiting, constipation, polyuria and polydipsia, and renal ultrasound shows nephrocalcinosis. Which is the most likely explanation?

  1. Persistent vitamin D deficiency
  2. Hypocalcemic tetany
  3. Vitamin D toxicity (hypervitaminosis D)
  4. Familial hypophosphatemic rickets
Answer: C. Prolonged vitamin D therapy → hypervitaminosis D: hypercalcemia with GI upset, polyuria/polydipsia, renal stones and nephrocalcinosis. Manage by stopping vit D and Ca, IV fluids, and steroids. Deficiency (A) would cause hypocalcemia, not hypercalcemia.

Red flags / contraindications
  • Live vaccines: immunocompromised, pregnancy.
  • Any vaccine: history of anaphylaxis to it.
  • Rotavirus: prior intussusception, SCID.
  • OPV: household immunocompromised contact (faecal-oral shedding).
  • DTaP: encephalopathy within 7 days of a prior dose = absolute C/I; defer in evolving neuro disorder <1 y.
MCQ pearls
  • MMR is live → defer in immunosuppression. BCG, OPV, Rota also live.
  • HBV vaccine + HBIG within 12 h for baby of HBsAg+ mother (efficacy 90–95%).
  • BCG (efficacy ~80%) chiefly prevents miliary TB & TB meningitis.
  • OPV gives intestinal/herd immunity (use in outbreaks) but risks vaccine-associated polio; IPV is the safe killed form.
  • Tetanus-prone wound: <3/unknown doses → toxoid + Ig; clean minor wound just needs toxoid if due.
  • MMR adverse: arthralgia 7–21 d, thrombocytopenia (measles), parotitis/orchitis (mumps).

Instruments & Procedures

Definition Common bedside airway, oxygen-delivery, vascular and sampling devices — examiners ask the single best indication, contraindication, or characteristic complication.
Airway & intubation
  • Endotracheal tube: uncuffed usual in neonates — cuffed causes subglottic stenosis. Right-bronchus advancement → lung collapse; oesophageal placement → abdominal distension + desaturation.
  • Laryngoscope blades: Miller (straight) — peds; Macintosh (curved) — older children/adults.
  • Oropharyngeal (Guedel) airway: unconscious patient only — no C/I, but provokes gag/vomiting if conscious.
  • Nasopharyngeal airway: tolerated even when conscious (tongue obstruction, trismus).
  • Laryngeal mask: rescue ventilation; C/I full stomach / aspiration risk.
  • Tracheostomy: for severe upper-airway obstruction or prolonged intubation; used when ETT impossible.
  • Ambu bag: positive-pressure ventilation in the non-breathing patient; C/I congenital diaphragmatic hernia; risk barotrauma/pneumothorax.

Tubes, drains & vascular access
  • NGT: length = nose → earlobe → midpoint of xiphoid–umbilicus. Confirm by air insufflation + epigastric auscultation, aspirate, or X-ray. C/I basal skull fracture, oesophageal varices, corrosive ingestion.
  • Chest tube: pneumothorax / effusion / empyema — 4th–5th ICS, mid-axillary line.
  • Foley catheter: C/I blood/pus per urethra (urethral trauma/urethritis).
  • Suprapubic aspiration: definitive UTI sample in child <2 y.
  • Intraosseous line: emergency access after failed IV — proximal tibia (2–3 cm below tuberosity); C/I osteogenesis imperfecta, ipsilateral fracture.
  • Umbilical vein catheter: emergency neonatal access & exchange transfusion; C/I omphalitis, NEC, omphalocele/gastroschisis.
  • Central line / bone marrow / paracentesis / pericardiocentesis — the classic complication is infection plus a local one (pneumothorax for central/chest, perforation for paracentesis, arrhythmia/tamponade for pericardiocentesis).
Inhalers & monitoring
  • MDI alone: needs coordination — poor if patient <5 y.
  • MDI + spacer: usable in all ages, especially <5 y; less oropharyngeal deposition; good in acute attacks.
  • Nebuliser: salbutamol, ipratropium, adrenaline, budesonide, hypertonic saline.
  • PEF meter: diagnose/monitor asthma severity — effort-dependent, unsuitable <5 y.
  • Urine dipstick: UTI, DKA, nephrotic monitoring — rapid bedside test.
MCQ pearls
  • Cuffed ETT in a neonate → subglottic stenosis (use uncuffed).
  • Miller = peds straight blade.
  • <5 y who can't coordinate an MDI → add a spacer.
  • UTI confirmation in <2 y → suprapubic aspiration; never diagnose UTI on a bag specimen C&S.
  • Chest tube site = 5th ICS mid-axillary line.

Definition Bug–drug matching: Gram stain → likely organism → spectrum class. Plus the signature toxicity of common chemo agents.

  • GBS (S. agalactiae) — early-onset neonatal sepsis & meningitis.

Pediatric History (reference structure)

Definition A paediatric history follows the adult skeleton but adds birth, developmental, nutritional, immunisation, and family/social sections — taken from the carer.
Sequence
  • General info — name, age, address, who history taken from, route of admission (ER/OPC).
  • Chief complaint (carer's words) + duration.
  • HPI — SOCRATES: site, onset, character, radiation, associated symptoms, timing/course, exacerbating/relieving, severity.
  • Systemic review — general, GIT, eyes, CVS/resp (cyanosis, sweating on feeding, stridor), ENT, skin (Mongolian spot), neuro, MSK, urinary.
  • Past medical/surgical, drug history & allergy.
  • Prenatal — mother's age, gestation, fever/rash/DM, radiation, PROM >18–24 h, drugs.
  • Natal/postnatal — delivery mode + why, birth weight, cried at birth, NICU, neonatal jaundice.
  • Nutritional — breast/bottle, weaning.
  • Developmental — the four streams + school performance.
  • Vaccination — type + age, complications.
  • Family — parental ages, consanguinity, genetic disease, early deaths.
  • Socioeconomic/psychosocial — smoking (passive/active), parental occupation, travel, contacts, mental health.
MCQ pearls
  • PROM >18–24 h is a key prenatal red flag (early-onset sepsis risk).
  • Excessive sweating during feeding = cardiac (heart-failure) screening question.
  • Consanguinity + early family deaths → think inherited/metabolic disease.
  • The four developmental streams must always be asked: gross motor, fine motor, language, social.

Test Yourself — Miscellaneous

Q1. A 9-month-old is brought for a check. He sits unsupported and crawls, but cries and clings when the doctor approaches and his mother steps back. He says “baba” without clearly meaning his father. Which finding is most appropriate for his age?

  1. Mature pincer grasp
  2. Separation anxiety
  3. Two-word sentences
  4. Riding a tricycle
Answer: B. Separation anxiety and non-specific “mama/dada” appear at 9 months. Mature pincer grasp and walking come at 12 m; tricycle and 3-word sentences at 3 y.

Q2. A 7-month-old, ex-term, has a persistent palmar grasp and Moro reflex, increased lower-limb tone with scissoring, and cannot sit. Which is the most likely explanation?

  1. Normal variation
  2. Benign delayed maturation
  3. Cerebral palsy
  4. Werdnig-Hoffmann (SMA)
Answer: C. Primitive reflexes should be gone by 3–6 m; their persistence with hypertonia and scissoring is the classic picture of cerebral palsy. SMA causes hypotonia, not scissoring.

Q3. A 4-month-old on chemotherapy for leukaemia is due his routine vaccines. Which scheduled vaccine is contraindicated?

  1. IPV (in the Hexa vaccine)
  2. Hib (in the Hexa vaccine)
  3. Hepatitis B
  4. Oral polio vaccine (OPV)
Answer: D. OPV is a live-attenuated vaccine, contraindicated in immunocompromise. IPV, Hib (killed) and HBV (recombinant) are safe.

Q4. A neonate born to an HBsAg-positive mother is delivered vaginally. What is the most appropriate immediate intervention?

  1. Hepatitis B vaccine + HBIG within 12 hours
  2. Hepatitis B vaccine alone at 2 months
  3. Lamivudine prophylaxis only
  4. No action; check serology at 9 months
Answer: A. Babies of HBsAg+ mothers need both active (vaccine) and passive (HBIG 0.5 mL) immunisation within 12 hours of birth.

Q5. A 2-month-old received his first DTaP-containing vaccine and developed an encephalopathy within 5 days, not attributable to another cause. Regarding future doses, the correct stance is:

  1. Give the next DTaP normally
  2. The pertussis component is an absolute contraindication to further DTaP
  3. Switch to oral polio vaccine instead
  4. Give DTaP but premedicate with paracetamol
Answer: B. Encephalopathy within 7 days of a prior dose, with no other cause, is an absolute contraindication to further DTaP. OPV is unrelated and does not substitute for DTaP.
